## 5P Content Framework — Proof. Pain. Promise. Plan. Picture.

A structure for content that engages and converts. Order matters.

1. **Proof** — establish credibility first. Achievements, client success, or data. Answers "why should I listen to you?"
2. **Pain** — name the problem clearly so it feels relatable and urgent. Earns attention.
3. **Promise** — state the tangible outcome or transformation the audience gets.
4. **Plan** — the roadmap / actionable steps to reach the promise. Makes the path feel attainable.
5. **Picture** — paint the payoff. Vivid story, metaphor, or visual of what success looks like.

Use it to bridge the psychological triggers that move someone from attention to action.

## Hook / Retain / Reward Framework

- **Hook:** earn the first few seconds with a strong claim, problem, or tension.
- **Retain:** keep attention with pace, story, and open loops.
- **Reward:** pay off the promise with a clear takeaway or win.

## StoryBrand (SB7) — framework only

Distilled from Building a StoryBrand 2.0 for application, not reproduction. The core idea: the customer is the hero, not the brand. The brand is the guide. Clarify your message around these seven elements.

1. **A character.** The customer is the hero. Lead with what *they* want, not what you offer. One clear desire, tied to survival/thriving (status, time, security, belonging).
2. **Has a problem.** Three layers: external (the tangible problem), internal (how it makes them feel), philosophical (why it's just plain wrong). Brands win by solving the internal problem, not just the external one.
3. **And meets a guide.** The guide shows two things: **empathy** ("I understand your problem") and **authority** (proof you can help: results, testimonials, stats, logos). The guide is not the hero.
4. **Who gives them a plan.** A simple, clear path (usually 3 steps) that removes confusion and risk. Names the steps to working with you.
5. **And calls them to action.** Direct CTA (buy, book, download) and a transitional CTA (free value that nurtures: a guide, a webinar). Always make the next step obvious.
6. **That helps them avoid failure.** Name the stakes. What does the hero lose if they don't act? A little fear, used honestly, raises urgency.
7. **And ends in success.** Paint the transformation. Show clearly how life looks after the problem is solved. Cast a vision of the new identity the hero becomes.

**Application to REACH:**
- The book's *reader* (the agent) is the hero. Brett, the author, is the guide: empathy ("I've watched good agents stay invisible") plus authority (building multimillion-dollar businesses since 2000, Five Star since 2011 and RTM since 2012; never a fixed year count).
- Inside the agent's own marketing, the *agent* is the guide and *their client* is the hero. This is a clean teaching point: stop being the hero of your content. (Ties directly to Chapter 8's "be the guide, not the hero.")
- Every chapter should resolve an internal problem, not just an external one. External: "I don't get enough listings." Internal: "I feel invisible and overlooked." Philosophical: "Good agents shouldn't lose to louder ones."

## Hormozi style guide (style mechanics to emulate, not text to copy)

Distilled from $100M Offers and Hormozi's general nonfiction style. Style isn't copyrightable; we emulate the mechanics, never reproduce his words. Observable patterns to write like:

- **One idea per paragraph.** Often one idea per *sentence*. Heavy use of line breaks and white space. (Matches the book rule: 3 sentences or fewer per paragraph.)
- **Open with a bold or contrarian claim**, then back it up. Earn the next line in the first line.
- **Short, punchy, declarative sentences.** Plain words. Low reading level on purpose. Cut adverbs and qualifiers.
- **Concrete over abstract.** Specific numbers, specific examples, specific dollar figures. "Show, don't tell."
- **Repetition for emphasis.** Restate the key idea in a tighter form. Use callbacks to earlier lines.
- **Name the objection, then crush it.** Anticipate what the reader is thinking and answer it directly.
- **Proof placement.** Drop proof (data, results, examples) right after a claim, before moving on.
- **Direct address.** Talk to "you." Conversational, second person, like advice from someone who's done it.
- **Aphoristic takeaways.** End sections with a short, memorable, quotable line. (Brett's draft already does this: "Volume negates luck," "Embrace the suck," "Nobody cares about real estate... until they do.")
- **Frameworks and lists as payoff,** not filler. Save the step-by-step for action sections; keep the body narrative.

**What to take vs leave:** take the cadence, structure, and directness. Leave Hormozi's profanity and aggressive bravado. Brett's voice is "humble confidence, advisory, friendly and fun" (per the project rules), so blend Hormozi's punch with Brett's guide-not-guru tone.
