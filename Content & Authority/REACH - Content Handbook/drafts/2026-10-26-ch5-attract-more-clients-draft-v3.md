## Nobody wakes up wanting an agent

[BRETT STORY, opening: a first-person moment, with dialogue, when Brett saw an agent lose (or win) a client months before the first call. Needs who, what was said, the stakes, the lesson.]

Picture a couple standing in their kitchen on a Sunday morning. Their second baby is due in the spring, and the spare bedroom is still the office. Nobody says, "We should sell the house." One of them says, "Where are we going to put the crib?"

That question is where a listing begins.

Over the next few months, they notice a sold sign down the street. A friend mentions what her house went for. They watch a short video from a local agent about what move-up families in their area can expect to pay. Then they watch another one. One night they pick up the phone and call an agent.

**Which agent do they call?**

Most agents never meet this couple until that call. Some never find out why they didn't get it. I want you to be the one who was in the room the whole time.

This chapter covers three things. First, how a person moves from noticing a problem to hiring an agent. Second, what your content does at each stop on that path. Third, how to know which stop a person is at, so you say the right thing at the right time.

## The call is the end of the process

In Chapter 4, you built an audience of the right people. Now we turn that audience into clients.

Here's the problem. Most agents write every post as if the reader is ready to sign today.

Look at the feed. You'll see a new listing, a just-sold, and a free home valuation. All of that is Decision content. Most of the people who follow you aren't in the market right now, so most of your audience isn't at the Decision stage at any given moment.

Now look at the stakes. According to the National Association of Realtors, 81% of sellers hire the first agent they talk to. The choice is mostly made in that first conversation. That means the work that wins it happens in the months before it, like the months that couple spent watching and wondering.

You can't create demand. People move when life moves. What you can do is be the guide they already trust when life moves them.

I define the **client journey** as the path a person takes from noticing a problem, to hiring you, to sending you the next person. Your content has a different job at each stop. The path has five stops: Awareness, Consideration, Decision, Retention, and Advocacy. We'll take the first three one at a time, then bridge to the last two.

## Why people choose you

What gets someone to choose you?

You show them you can solve their problem, and that your way of doing it gets a better result than the alternatives. The key word is *show*. "I get great results" is a claim, and every agent makes it.

Showing looks like a clear process, real stories, and specific outcomes in your clients' own words.

Along the way, you are the guide. A guide has empathy ("I understand what you're dealing with") and authority ("I've helped people through this"). Good content at every stage does at least one of the two.

## Stage 1: Awareness

You don't wake up wanting a new car. Your car starts making a noise, and one day you realize you have a problem.

Homeowners and renters hear the noise too. The house feels smaller every year. The rent keeps climbing. The kids are leaving, and nobody uses the upstairs. A job offer comes in from three states away.

**At the Awareness stage, a person becomes aware of a problem that's keeping them from getting what they want.** They don't call it a real estate problem yet. They just know something doesn't fit.

### What your content does

Your content helps them name the problem and see what life looks like when it's fixed. The test is simple: **would someone feel understood after reading this?** If yes, the piece works.

Here are three examples that work in any market:

- **A signs-it's-time post.** A short video on the signs a home has stopped fitting a family's life, like the spare room that became storage or the commute that keeps getting longer. Each sign names a problem the viewer may already have.
- **A plain-English market note.** What happened to homes like theirs on their street this year, and what it means for them. It answers the question every owner quietly asks: "What is my home worth to me?"
- **A neighbor story.** A family a few streets over who moved closer to grandparents, and how they decided it was time. Ask their permission first.

The neighbor story works because of the peer effect. When people see someone like them solve the same problem, they get curious. That curiosity should feel like an invitation, never like pressure.

Keep the urgency honest. Real urgency is a true reason to act soon, like a season, a rate change, or a deadline in their life. If you invent urgency, you lose the one thing a guide has, which is trust.

### Where this lives in your spheres

Awareness content mostly works on your Sphere of Proximity and your Sphere of Recognition. Most of it carries no ask at all.

**Bottom line:** People who don't know they have a problem can't hire you to fix it. Your job here is to help them see it.

**Action step:** Sort your last 20 posts into Awareness, Consideration, and Decision, and count how many are Awareness.

## Stage 2: Consideration

Now the couple has a name for the problem. They don't know what to do about it, so they start exploring.

**At the Consideration stage, a person begins looking for ways to fix the problem.** They have two kinds of questions. How does this even work? And who would I trust to help me?

### What your content does

Your content shows you're the most effective solution. You do it in two moves, and the order matters.

First, **educate them on what makes an effective approach.** What does a strong pricing strategy look like? What should they get ready before they list? What happens in the first month? Teach it as if you were helping a friend, even if they never hire you.

Second, **prove you deliver that approach better, faster, and easier than the options they're weighing.** Better is the outcome. Faster and easier is the experience of working with you.

Education comes first for a reason. A person who doesn't understand the process chooses on gut feel. Gut feel favors the familiar name or the cheapest price.

### Remove the uncertainty

Three things take the fear out of the unknown.

- **Process clarity.** Show what working with you looks like, step by step: the first call, how you price, how you market, how you handle the closing. When people can see the path, it feels safer to start walking.
- **Quality outcomes.** Tell client stories that start with what the client wanted and end with what they got. "They got to move closer to family" lands harder than a number. When a number matters, use the real one.
- **Client satisfaction.** Your clients say it better than you do. Reviews, short video testimonials, and thank-you notes (with permission) carry weight your own description can't.

This is also where leads show up. Someone replies to your email or asks a question under a video. A good response is quick, answers the question they asked, and ends with one good question back.

### Where this lives in your spheres

Consideration is the home of the Sphere of Recognition. These people know your face or your name. They haven't decided you're their agent yet. Education and proof move them from "I know of them" to "I'd call them first."

**Bottom line:** Teach first, then prove. A person who understands the process and has seen your proof stops comparing and starts choosing.

**Action step:** Start a proof folder and save every kind word, review, and finished outcome in it the day it arrives, with permission.

## Stage 3: Decision

Eventually, the couple decides. They're going to sell, and they're going to do it this year. One question is left.

**At the Decision stage, a person has decided to fix the problem and is choosing who will help.** This is the stage most agents talk to all the time. It's also the one stage where a clear ask belongs.

[BRETT STORY: a time he watched an agent lose a person at the Decision stage because the offer or next step was unclear.]

### What your content does

Your content offers to fix the problem and makes it easy to say yes. Three pieces make that work.

**Make the offer crystal clear.** What do you do, for whom, and what happens next? "Let's chat" leaves people guessing. "A 30-minute conversation about your plan, a walk through your home, and a written pricing strategy at the end" is an offer.

**Show proof of value.** Give a sample of what they'll get, like a sample marketing plan, a sample pricing analysis, or a short video of how you prepare a home. Show, don't tell, applied to your own work.

**Make it easy to book.** One link and one clear next step. Every extra step is another chance for them to drop off.

### Add a reason to act now

The three classic levers are urgency, exclusivity, and added value. Only the true ones hold up.

True urgency is a market window or a date in their life. True exclusivity is a limit on your capacity, because you can only serve so many listings well at once. Added value is a bonus that helps, like a pre-listing checklist or a staging consult. The couple can feel an invented reason, and so can everyone they tell.

### Lead, prospect, client

Three words track a person through this stage.

- **Lead.** Someone with a problem you can solve who has shown interest, like a reply, a question, or a message.
- **Prospect.** A lead who wants *you* to solve it and meets your criteria. You get to have criteria, like a realistic timeline and a home in the area you serve best.
- **Client.** Someone who hired you.

Your content moves people from the first to the third.

### Where this lives in your spheres

Decision is mostly the Sphere of Influence. These people are often already convinced and need a clear, easy way to call you. They also pass your Decision content to friends who are ready.

**Bottom line:** A person who has decided needs a clear offer and an easy next step. Make both obvious.

**Action step:** Write your offer in one sentence: what you do, for whom, and the next step.

## Retention and Advocacy: the bridge to Chapter 6

The journey doesn't end when the keys change hands. Two more stops come after Decision.

**Retention** is the client who keeps choosing you for every transaction after the first. **Advocacy** is the client who tells other people to choose you too.

An advocate does for someone else what you did in the first three stages. They name the problem and recommend a guide they trust. That's why referrals are so powerful. A friend's recommendation skips Awareness and half of Consideration. Remember the number from Chapter 4: 66% of listings come from referrals and previous clients.

Each advocate's story becomes Awareness content for the next family in the kitchen. That's the **referral flywheel**, and it's the subject of the next chapter.

**Bottom line:** The first sale is the start of the next one.

**Action step:** Make a list of your last five clients and decide today how each one will hear from you in the next 30 days.

## Give until they ask

How much of your content should ask for something? Alex Hormozi gives a benchmark in *$100M Leads*. TV runs about 13 minutes of ads in every 60, which is roughly 3.5 pieces of value for every ask. Facebook runs about 4 content posts for every ad. Growing platforms give far more than that. His own recommendation is to "give until they ask."

Use that as a floor. Keep at least 4 gives for every ask, and give more while your audience is still small.

## What life looks like when this works

Go back to the kitchen and picture it going well. The couple has watched your market notes for months. They saw the neighbor story, and they watched your walk-through of how a listing gets prepared. When they were ready, they sent you a message.

By the time you sit down at their table, you're finishing a conversation you started a long time ago.

More of your calls come from warm people who already trust you. You chase less. You spend fewer hours on appointments with people who were never going to hire anyone. Your feed starts to feel like help. *(Inferred outcome, not a measured result.)*

## Objections

**"People don't move through neat stages. They jump around."** They do. A friend can send someone straight to Decision, and someone else can sit in Awareness for two years. The stages are a check that you have something for each kind of person.

**"If I teach people how it works, won't they do it themselves or go cheaper?"** Some will, and they were never going to hire you. Most people don't want to become experts in real estate. They want to hand the problem to someone who is. When you teach well, you show what a good agent does.

**"Won't Awareness content feel like manufacturing fear?"** Only if you manufacture it. Describe a real problem that real people have, in plain words, and offer a better picture. If you'd be uncomfortable explaining a post to the person who sees it, rewrite it.

**"I'm newer. I don't have results to show yet."** Use process clarity. Record a two-minute video on the questions you ask at a first listing conversation, or how you decide on a price. That shows how you think without a single sale to point to. Add client voices as you collect them.

**"This sounds like three times the content."** It isn't. One transaction becomes a content run, like we covered in Chapter 4, and each part of the run serves a different stage. We'll build the full plan in Chapter 7.

## Action items

Do these in order. Each one fits in a single sitting.

1. **Count your stages.**
   - How to do it: Label your last 20 posts Awareness, Consideration, or Decision.
   - Why it's important: Most agents find nearly all of them are Decision content, which reaches the smallest part of the audience at any one time.
   - Example: A new-listing post and a just-sold post are both Decision.

2. **Write your qualified-client criteria.**
   - How to do it: List three or four traits of the people you serve best.
   - Why it's important: You can't tell a lead from a prospect without it.
   - Example: Planning to list within six months, in neighborhoods you know best.

3. **Write your offer in one sentence.**
   - How to do it: Say what you do, for whom, and the next step.
   - Why it's important: A clear offer is the heart of Decision content.
   - Example: "A 30-minute conversation about your plan, a walk through your home, and a written pricing strategy at the end."

4. **Map one idea to each stage.**
   - How to do it: Pick one question your clients ask. Make one piece for Awareness, one for Consideration, and one for Decision.
   - Why it's important: You see how one idea serves the whole journey.
   - Example: "When is the right time to sell?" as a signs post, a pros-and-cons walk-through, and an invitation to talk about their timeline.

### The journey at a glance

| Stage | What the person is thinking | What your content does | Who you mostly reach | Ask? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Awareness | "Something isn't working." | Names the problem and shows a better life | SOP and SOR | No |
| Consideration | "How do I fix this, and who can help?" | Educates, then proves you do it better, faster, easier | SOR moving toward SOI | Light |
| Decision | "I'm ready. Who do I call?" | Clear offer, proof of value, easy booking | SOI and warm leads | Yes |
| Retention and Advocacy | "That went well." | Keeps you first in mind and gives them a story to share | Past clients | Chapter 6 |
