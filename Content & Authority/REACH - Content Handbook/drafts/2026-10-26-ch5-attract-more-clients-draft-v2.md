## Strategy Box

- **Angle:** Nobody wakes up wanting an agent. They wake up with a problem, and the agent who guided them through it is the one they call. Content is how you guide a person through each stage of the client journey before the first conversation happens.
- **Frameworks:** The client journey (Awareness, Consideration, Decision, with Retention and Advocacy as a bridge). The Three Spheres from Chapter 4. StoryBrand (Donald Miller): the client is the hero, you are the guide. 5P (Proof, Pain, Promise, Plan, Picture) woven through.
- **Takeaway:** "I can match my content to where a person is on their journey, and pull them toward a conversation with me."

## Nobody wakes up wanting an agent

Picture a couple standing in their kitchen on a Sunday morning. Their second baby is due in the spring, and the spare bedroom is still the office. Nobody says, "We should sell the house." One of them says, "Where are we going to put the crib?"

That question is where a listing begins.

Over the next few months, they notice a sold sign down the street. A friend mentions what her house went for. They watch a short video from a local agent explaining what families moving up in their area can expect to pay, and then they watch another one. One night they pick up the phone and call an agent.

**Which agent do they call?**

Most agents never meet this couple until that call. Some never find out why they didn't get it. I want you to be the one who was in the room the whole time.

## Why the call is the end of the process

In Chapter 4, you built an audience of the right people and sorted them by how well they know you. Now we turn that audience into clients. Here's the problem: most agents write every post as if the reader is ready to sign today.

Look at the feed again, and you'll see posts announcing a new listing, a just-sold, and a free home valuation. All of that is Decision content. As we covered in Chapter 4, most of the people who follow you aren't in the market right now, so most of your audience isn't at the Decision stage at any given moment.

Remember what the National Association of Realtors found: 81% of sellers list with the first agent they speak to. My read of that number is that the first conversation is usually the moment of truth. If the choice is mostly made there, then the work that wins it happens in the months before it, like the months that couple spent watching and wondering.

And remember what we said about demand. You can't create it, because people move when life moves. What you can do is be the guide they already trust when life moves them.

I define the **client journey** as the path a person takes from noticing a problem, to hiring you, to sending you the next person. Your content has a different job at each stop on that path.

## Two questions, not one

You now have two ways to look at everyone in your audience, and they answer different questions.

The Three Spheres answer, "How well does this person know me?" The journey answers, "Where is this person with their problem?" A close friend in your SOI can be at the very start of the journey. A stranger in your SOP can be ready to call someone tomorrow.

The two still line up in a useful way. People in your Sphere of Proximity are mostly at the start, or before it. People in your Sphere of Recognition are often exploring, and they already know your name. People in your Sphere of Influence are the ones most likely to call you the moment they decide.

## The guide, the hero, and the three people on the path

The couple in the kitchen is the hero of this story. You are the guide. A guide shows empathy ("I understand what you're dealing with") and authority ("I've helped people through this"). Content at every stage does one of those two things, and the best content does both.

Along the journey, the same person plays three roles for you:

- **Lead.** Someone with a problem you can solve who has shown interest in solving it. They asked a question, answered a poll, replied to your email, or sent you a message.
- **Prospect.** Someone who expresses genuine interest in having *you* solve the problem and who meets your criteria. In other words, they're qualified, and you'd be glad to work together.
- **Client.** Someone who hired you.

A lead has raised a hand, and a prospect has chosen to walk toward you. Your content is what moves people from one to the other.

## Why people choose you

What gets someone to choose you?

The answer is that you show them you can solve their problem, and that your way of doing it gets them a better result than the alternatives. The key word is *show*. Saying "I get great results" is a claim, and every agent makes it.

Showing looks like a clear process, real stories, and specific outcomes in your clients' own words.

## Stage 1: Awareness

You don't wake up wanting a new car. Your car starts making a noise, and one day you realize you have a problem.

Home owners and renters get the noise too. The house feels smaller every year. The rent keeps climbing. The kids are leaving, and nobody uses the upstairs. A job offer comes in from three states away.

**At the Awareness stage, a person becomes aware of a problem that's keeping them from getting what they want.** They may not call it a real estate problem yet. They just know something doesn't fit.

### What your content is trying to do

Your objective is to help them name the problem and see what life looks like when it's fixed. You're helping the people who already feel the noise understand what it means.

The best Awareness content makes the problem easy to see and the better life easy to picture. A good test is this: **would someone feel understood after reading this?** If the answer is yes, the piece is doing its job.

Here are a few examples that work in any market:

- **A signs-it's-time post.** A short video on the signs a home has stopped fitting a family's life, such as the spare room that became storage or the commute that keeps getting longer. Each sign names a problem the viewer may already have.
- **A plain-English market note.** What happened to homes like theirs on their street or in their neighborhood this year, and what it could mean for them. Plain words work best, and they answer the question every owner quietly asks, "What is my home worth to me?"
- **A neighbor story.** A family a few streets over who moved closer to grandparents, or finally got the extra room, and how they decided it was time. It helps to ask their permission first.

That last one is where the peer effect comes in. When people see someone like them solve the same problem, they get curious. Some people call that FOMO, but I'd rather call it curiosity, because it should feel like an invitation and never like pressure.

### Keep the urgency honest

The classic advice is to make the pain feel urgent enough that people go looking for a solution. I agree with the goal, but I'd define urgency differently. Real urgency is a true reason to act soon, such as a season, a rate change, or a deadline in their life.

If you invent urgency, you risk the one thing a guide has, which is trust. That's hard to earn back.

### Where this lives in your spheres

Awareness content mostly works on your Sphere of Proximity and your Sphere of Recognition. These are the people who aren't close to you yet. They're also the people who may refer you to someone else who's feeling the noise.

This is the "expand" half of the binary strategy from Chapter 4. Most of your Awareness content can carry no ask at all.

## Stage 2: Consideration

Now the couple in the kitchen has a name for the problem. They're not sure what to do about it. So they start exploring.

**At the Consideration stage, a person begins looking for ways to fix the problem.** They have questions, and the questions come in two kinds. How does this even work? And who would I trust to help me?

### What your content is trying to do

Your objective is to show that you're the most effective solution. You do this in two moves, and the order matters.

First, **educate them on what makes an effective approach.** What does a strong pricing strategy look like? What should they get ready before they list? What happens in the first month? Teach it as if you were helping a friend, even if they never hire you.

Second, **prove that you deliver that approach better, faster, and easier than the options they're weighing.** Better means the outcomes. Faster and easier mean the experience of working with you.

Education comes first for a reason, and this part is my opinion, not a research finding. Uncertainty can push people toward the agent they already know, or toward the cheapest one. A person who doesn't understand the process may choose on gut feel, and gut feel tends to favor the familiar name.

### Remove the uncertainty

Three things take the fear out of the unknown: process clarity, quality outcomes, and client satisfaction.

- **Process clarity.** Show what working with you looks like, step by step. That includes the first call, how you prepare the home, how you price it, how you market it, and how you handle the closing. When people can see the path, it feels safer to start walking.
- **Quality outcomes.** Tell client stories that start with what the client wanted and end with what they got. "They got to move closer to family" lands harder than a number. When a number matters, the real one is the one to use.
- **Client satisfaction.** Your clients can say it better than you can. Reviews, short video testimonials, and thank-you notes (with permission) carry weight that your own description can't.

This is also where leads show up. A lead might reply to your email, ask a question under a video, or request a guide. A good response is quick, answers the question they asked, and ends with one good question back.

### Where this lives in your spheres

Consideration is the home of the Sphere of Recognition. These people already know your face or your name. They just haven't decided you're their agent yet.

In Chapter 4, I said your job with this group is to earn the position of being their agent before they need one. This is how you finish the job. Education and proof move a person from "I know of them" to "I'd call them first."

## Stage 3: Decision

Eventually, the couple decides. They're going to sell, and they're going to do it this year. Now there's one question left.

**At the Decision stage, a person has decided to fix the problem and is choosing who will help.** This is the moment most agents are talking to all the time, and it's the one stage where a clear ask belongs.

### What your content is trying to do

Your objective is to offer to fix the problem and make it easy to say yes today. That sounds simple, and it's easy to get wrong. Three pieces make it work.

**Make the offer crystal clear.** What exactly do you do, for whom, and what happens next? A line like "Let's chat" leaves people guessing what they'd get. "A 30-minute conversation about your plan, a walk through your home, and a written pricing strategy at the end" is an offer.

**Show proof of value.** Give a sample of what they'll get, such as a sample marketing plan, a sample pricing analysis, or a short video of how you prepare a home. This is the "show, don't tell" idea again, applied to your own work.

**Make it easy to book.** A single link and a single clear next step make it easy to say yes. As a general marketing principle, every extra step adds friction, and friction gives people another chance to drop off.

### Add a reason to act now

The last piece is a reason to move. The three classic levers are urgency, exclusivity, and added value, and the ones that hold up are the true ones.

True urgency is a market window or a date in their life. True exclusivity is a limit on your capacity, because you can only serve so many listings well at once. Added value is a bonus that actually helps them, like a pre-listing checklist or a staging consult.

If an invented reason ever feels tempting, remember that the couple can usually feel it, and so can everyone they tell.

### Know who is qualified

Remember the prospect, the person who wants you and meets your criteria. You get to have criteria. They're a way to protect your time and theirs.

Your criteria might include a realistic timeline, a willingness to follow your pricing advice, and a home in the area and price range you serve best. Qualifying works in both directions, because the right clients are checking you too. When both sides say yes, the work is better for everyone.

### Where this lives in your spheres

Decision is mostly where your Sphere of Influence lives. When someone in your SOI decides to move, they're often already convinced and mainly need a clear, easy way to call you.

Those same people can also pass your Decision content to friends who are ready. That's why it helps to keep an offer visible even for a warm audience, as long as most of what you post still gives value and asks for nothing.

## Retention and Advocacy: a short bridge

The journey doesn't end when the keys change hands. Two more stages come after Decision, and they're the reason this whole system compounds.

**Retention** is the client who keeps choosing you for every transaction after the first. **Advocacy** is the client who tells other people to choose you too. When your advocates talk, they do for someone else what you did in the first three stages. They name the problem and recommend a guide they trust.

That's one reason referrals are so powerful. In my reading, a friend's recommendation skips Awareness and half of Consideration, because someone the person already trusts did the guiding. Remember the number from Chapter 4: 66% of listings come from referrals and previous clients.

Each advocate's story becomes Awareness content for the next family in the kitchen. That's the **referral flywheel**, and it's the subject of the next chapter.

## What life can look like when this works

Go back to the kitchen one more time, and picture it going well. The couple has watched your market notes for months. They saw the neighbor story, and they watched your walk-through of how a listing gets prepared. When they were ready, they sent you a message.

By the time you sit down at their table, you may be finishing a conversation you started a long time ago.

That's the possibility in front of you. More of your calls can come from warm people who already trust you. You may spend less time chasing, and less time on appointments with people who were never going to hire anyone. Your feed can start to feel like help.

## What the evidence says

I want to be careful here. The journey is a model, so it's worth testing in your own market. The solid facts are the two we've already used. According to the National Association of Realtors, 66% of listings come from referrals and previous clients, and 81% of sellers hire the first agent they talk to.

Together, they point, in my reading, to the same conclusion. Most of the winning happens before the first conversation, and most of it comes through people who already know you. Everything else in this chapter is my reasoning on top of those facts.

## Objections

**"People don't move through neat stages. They jump around."** They do, and you're right. Someone can skip Consideration entirely because a friend sent them to you, and someone else can sit in Awareness for two years. The stages work as a check that you have something for each kind of person.

**"If I teach people how it works, won't they do it themselves or go cheaper?"** Some will, and those people were never going to hire you. My view is that most people don't want to become experts in real estate. They want to feel safe handing the problem to someone who is. When you teach well, you show what a good agent does, and that makes the choice of a skilled one easier.

**"Won't Awareness content feel like manufacturing fear?"** It does when it's done that way. A better standard is to describe a real problem that real people have, in plain words, and offer a better picture. A piece you'd be uncomfortable explaining to the viewer is probably worth a second look.

**"I'm newer. I don't have results to show yet."** Then process clarity can carry the load. For example, a newer agent can record a two-minute video on the questions they ask at a first listing conversation, or how they decide on a price. That shows how they think without a single sale to point to. Client voices can be added as you collect them, so your proof stays real.

**"This sounds like three times the content."** It doesn't have to be. One transaction can become a content run, like we covered in Chapter 4, and each part of that run can serve a different stage. We'll build the actual plan in Chapter 7.

## Action items

These work best in order, and each is small enough to finish in one sitting.

1. **Sort your last 20 posts by stage.**
   - How to do it: Label each post Awareness, Consideration, or Decision.
   - Why it's important: You may find that most of them are Decision content, which reaches the smallest part of your audience at any one time.
   - Example: A new listing post and a just-sold post are both Decision content.

2. **Write your qualified-client criteria.**
   - How to do it: List three or four traits of the people you serve best, such as timeline, price range, and willingness to follow your advice.
   - Why it's important: You can't tell a lead from a prospect without it.
   - Example: Planning to list within six months, in the neighborhoods you know best.

3. **Write your offer in one sentence.**
   - How to do it: Say what you do, for whom, and the next step, in plain words.
   - Why it's important: A clear offer is the heart of Decision content.
   - Example: "A 30-minute conversation about your plan, a walk through your home, and a written pricing strategy."

4. **Map one idea to each stage.**
   - How to do it: Pick one question your clients ask, then make one piece for Awareness, one for Consideration, and one for Decision.
   - Why it's important: You'll see how one idea can serve the whole journey.
   - Example: "When is the right time to sell?" as a signs post, a pros-and-cons walk-through, and an invitation to talk about their timeline.

5. **Start a proof folder.**
   - How to do it: Save every kind word, review, and finished outcome in one place, with permission.
   - Why it's important: Proof is what makes Consideration content work.
   - Example: A screenshot of a client message, saved the day it arrives.

| Stage | What the person is thinking | What your content does | Who you mostly reach | Ask? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Awareness | "Something isn't working." | Names the problem and shows a better life | SOP and SOR | No |
| Consideration | "How do I fix this, and who can help?" | Educates, then proves you do it better, faster, easier | SOR moving toward SOI | Light |
