BEAT 1: He Was a Friend First ~2 min

The point: The partnerships that go wrong rarely start with a stranger. They start with someone you like, trust, and see yourself in, and that is why people skip the hard conversations.
Story beat: You hired him as a salesperson on a consulting project in Beverly Hills. You worked together for a couple of months, became friends, and stayed in touch because you were both entrepreneurial and very motivated. A couple of years later he started a wireless business with a retail kiosk in a mall, with a partner. They wanted to expand, were having a hard time getting into new retail locations, and asked if you'd join. You came in as a one-third partner, and that moved to 50/50 after you bought out the other partner. Keep the mall unnamed and the partner unnamed.
Toss: "Danny, Valli, have either of you gone into business with a friend? What made it feel safe?" Let them answer before you go on.
Land it: Because we were close friends, I took it for granted and never finalized my partnership agreement.
REEL LINE: "We were close friends, so I took it for granted and never finalized my partnership agreement."

BEAT 2: The Good Years ~1.5 min (optional, cut first if you run long)

The point: It was working, and that matters. This was not a bad business that fell apart. It was a great one, which is why the ending hurts the way it does. (Framing inferred from your account.)
Story beat: In 18 months you secured 7 other locations. The team went from 6 employees to over 70, activations went from 60 a month to 600 to 900, and the company was doing $3.5 million to $4 million a year in sales. Split the work this way: he built the outside sales team, staffed the stores, and trained the managers to sell. You ran the corporate office, hired the executive team and the operations staff, and built all of the infrastructure. Say "we" for the growth, since it was a team result, and "I" for your side of the split.
Toss: "Valli, when a business is growing that fast, what stops getting looked at?" Let her answer.
Land it: Nobody reads the paperwork when the numbers are going up. (Inferred; confirm it matches how you see it.)
REEL LINE: "When a partnership is working, nobody checks the paperwork, and that's exactly when the paperwork matters most." (Inferred from your lesson on contracts; keep only if it is how you'd say it.)

BEAT 3: Where It Broke ~2 min

The point: The break did not come all at once. It started as friction, turned into a buyout, and the buyout turned into a discovery.
Story beat: About 15 months in, he started working out of the office, and that's where the friction began. You tried to resolve it, could not coexist, and he agreed to buy you out. The terms were a structured payout based on the revenue the company was producing. Because you had built all of the infrastructure, you were the one confirming your own payouts, and you found he was not paying what you were owed. In your words, he was stealing from you. You confronted him, he tried to deny it, and that's when you went the legal route, which took four and a half years to resolve. (The wording about him on camera is your call.)
Toss: "Valli, you find out the number's wrong. What's the first thing you do?" Then to Danny: "Do you confront, or do you document first?" Let the two of them disagree.
Land it: I found out because I had built the system that tracked it. I just didn't have an agreement that protected me. (Second sentence inferred from your lesson on contracts.)
REEL LINE: "I built the infrastructure, so I was the one confirming my own payouts, and that's how I found out he wasn't paying what I was owed."

BEAT 4: The Lowest Point and the Anger ~3 min (the heart of the segment)

The point: Lead with the human cost before any lesson. The money was real, but the anger was the bigger loss.
Story beat: Tell your ONE specific moment first (see the note above). Then fill in the context from your account. After the lawsuit was filed, they stopped paying you entirely. You were using that buyout revenue to fund your own business-to-business wireless company, so the money you counted on to pay staff, rent for the office, and product disappeared. You laid off staff and broke your lease, which brought a second lawsuit, and you went through some very difficult financial times. Economically, the lawsuit cost you somewhere between $750,000 and $1 million that you never recovered. Then slow down for the part that matters most: you were so angry that you allowed it to consume you. All you wanted was to win and get revenge. Carrying that much anger for that long made it virtually impossible to move forward with your life and build a successful business. Only after the lawsuit ended and the company went bankrupt could you look back and see how much more it had cost you than what you were owed. You said "I allowed it." Say that.
Toss: Do not toss right away. Let the silence sit, then to both: "Have you ever been so right about something that being right started costing you?"
Planned friction: Danny pushes: "He was stealing from you. Fighting back isn't wrong, is it?" Brett's answer: No, and you did fight. But the fight cost $750,000 to $1 million you never recovered, and it took years of your life that you spent angry instead of building. The lesson is not "don't stand up for yourself." It is to count the full cost before you start and to know that anger will try to run the fight. Let it go 20 seconds before you resolve it. (Your account does not say the lawsuit was won or lost, so do not claim a result.)
Land it: It cost me far more than what I was owed, and I only saw that once it was over.
REEL LINE: "The lawsuit cost me somewhere between $750,000 and $1 million, and the anger cost me more."

BEAT 5: What I Took Forward ~3 min

The point: Three lessons, in the order you learned them. Say them as what you do now, not as rules for them.
Story beat and the three lessons:
1. Contracts. We were close friends, so I never finalized my partnership agreement. Their legal position was that I was never actually a partner, which we ultimately overcame. Every single contingency needs to be thought out and documented in an operating agreement.
2. Avoid lawsuits and litigation at all costs. They are incredibly expensive, financially and emotionally.
3. Partnerships are like marriages. If you rush in too quickly, there's a high chance it won't end well. What matters most is compatibility and complementary skill sets. Each person has to bring something of significant value to the table, because equity is based on contribution.
Toss: Danny on lesson 3: "You've said you won't take a cut of someone's commission unless you're fully invested in their career. Is that the same idea as equity based on contribution?" Valli on lesson 1: "You work with family in business. Is everyone's role written down?"
Planned friction: Valli pushes: "Isn't asking a friend to sign something kind of insulting?" Brett's answer: that is exactly the reasoning that kept your agreement unfinished, and it was the most expensive thing you took for granted. A clear agreement between friends protects the friendship. (Last sentence inferred from lessons 1 and 3.) Let them go back and forth before you close.
Land it: Put it in writing before you need it, stay out of court if you can, and bring something real to the table. Then the partnership has a chance.
REEL LINE: "Partnerships are like marriages. If you rush in too fast, there's a good chance they won't end well, and equity should be based on contribution."
