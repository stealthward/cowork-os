BEAT 1: He Started as a Friend ~2 min
The point: The agreement never got finalized because we were close friends.
Cues:
- Hired him as a salesperson on a consulting project, Beverly Hills
- Worked together a couple of months, until the project ended
- Became friends, stayed in touch: both entrepreneurial, very motivated
- Couple of years later, he started a wireless business, mall kiosk, with a partner
- They struggled to get into new retail locations, asked Brett to join
- One-third partner, then 50/50 after buying out the other partner
- Never finalized the partnership agreement
Toss: "Danny, Valli, have either of you gone into business with a friend? What made it feel safe?"
Land it: Close friends, so I took it for granted.
REEL LINE: "We were close friends, so I took it for granted and never finalized my partnership agreement."

BEAT 2: The Growth ~1.5 min (cut first if long)
The point: The business was growing fast.
Cues:
- 18 months, 7 other locations secured
- 6 employees to over 70
- 60 activations a month to 600 to 900
- $3.5 million to $4 million a year in sales
- His side: outside sales team, staffing stores, training managers
- Brett's side: corporate office, executive team, operations staff, all the infrastructure
Toss: "Valli, Danny, what does fast growth do to the conversations a partnership should be having?"
Land it: We were growing, and the agreement was still unfinished.
REEL LINE: "In 18 months we went from 6 employees to over 70, and from 60 activations a month to 600 to 900."

BEAT 3: Where It Broke ~2 min
The point: A payout I was confirming myself showed me something was wrong.
Cues:
- About 15 months in, he started working out of the office, friction began
- Tried to resolve it, could not coexist
- He agreed to buy Brett out: structured payout based on company revenue
- Brett built all the infrastructure, so he confirmed his own payouts
- Discovered he was not paying what Brett was owed
- Brett's words: "He was stealing from me."
- Confronted him, he tried to deny it
- Went the legal route, 4.5 years to resolve
Toss: "Valli, you find out the number's wrong. What's the first thing you do?" Then Danny: "Do you confront, or document first?" Let them disagree.
Land it: I confronted him, he tried to deny it, and that's when we went the legal route.
REEL LINE: "I built all the infrastructure, so I was confirming my own payouts, and I discovered he wasn't paying what I was owed."

BEAT 4: The Lowest Point and the Anger ~3 min (the heart)
The point: The emotional toll was the bigger cost.
Cues:
- Lowest-point moment (the one Brett picked)
- After the lawsuit was filed, they stopped paying entirely
- Buyout revenue was funding Brett's own business-to-business wireless company
- Pay for staff, office rent, product: gone
- Laid off staff
- Broke the lease, which brought a second lawsuit
- Very difficult financial times
- $750,000 to $1 million, never recovered
- "More importantly": an incredible emotional toll
- So angry that "I allowed it to consume me"
- All he wanted: to win and get revenge
- That much anger, that long: virtually impossible to move forward and build a successful business
- Only after the lawsuit ended and the company went bankrupt could he look back
- Saw it cost far more than what he was owed financially
Toss: After a pause, to both: "Have you ever been so right about something that being right started costing you?"
Planned friction: Danny: "He was stealing from you. Fighting back isn't wrong, is it?" Brett's answer cues: he did fight; $750,000 to $1 million never recovered; years spent angry. Let it run 20 seconds before resolving.
Land it: It cost me far more than what I was owed.
REEL LINE: "I was so angry that I allowed it to consume me, and it cost me far more than the money I was owed."

BEAT 5: What I Pulled Forward ~3 min
The point: Three lessons, in the order I learned them.
Cues:
1. Contracts
- Close friends, so I never finalized my partnership agreement
- Their legal position: I was never actually a partner (we overcame it)
- Every contingency thought out and documented in an operating agreement
2. Avoid lawsuits and litigation at all costs
- Incredibly expensive, financially and emotionally
3. Partnerships are like marriages
- Rush in too quickly, high chance they won't end well
- Compatibility and complementary skill sets
- Each brings something of significant value
- Equity is based on contribution
Toss: Danny: "How do you think about equity in a partnership? What has to be true before you take a piece of someone else's commission?" Valli: "Your family started a mortgage company in the 1980s and you've been your mother's boss. How do roles and agreements work when it's family?"
Planned friction: Valli: "Isn't asking a friend to sign something kind of insulting?" Brett's answer cues: that is why mine never got finalized; their position was that I was never a partner. Let them go back and forth before closing.
Land it: Now I get every contingency documented in an operating agreement, I avoid litigation, and I look for compatibility and complementary skills before I partner.
REEL LINE: "Partnerships are like marriages. If you rush into them too quickly, there's a high chance they won't end well, and equity should be based on contribution."
