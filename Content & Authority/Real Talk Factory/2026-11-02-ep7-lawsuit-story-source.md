## The setup

The partnership started with someone who I had hired on a consulting project in Beverly Hills. I hired him as a salesperson, and we worked together for a couple of months until the project was over. We became friends and stayed in touch because we were very similar in that we were entrepreneurial and very motivated.

A couple of years later, he started a wireless business and opened a wireless retail kiosk in a mall in Orange County. He started this with a partner. They were attempting to expand the business and having difficulty getting into new retail locations, and asked if I would consider joining the partnership. I took on the role as a partner, initially as a one-third partner, and that moved up to a 50/50 partnership after we bought out the other partner.

Over the course of 18 months, I was able to secure 7 other locations, and we grew from 6 employees to over 70 and from 60 activations a month to 600 to 900 per month, doing $3.5 million to $4 million a year in sales. My partner was responsible for building the outside sales team, staffing the stores, and training the managers how to sell our products and services. I worked out of the corporate office, hired the executive team and all of the operations staff, and built out all of the infrastructure.

About 15 months into it, my partner started working out of the office, and that's where the friction began. We attempted to resolve it but could not coexist, and he agreed to buy me out. The buyout terms were a structured payout based upon the revenue that the company was providing. Because I had built all the infrastructure, I was confirming all of my payouts and discovered that he was not paying what I was owed. He was stealing from me. I confronted him. He tried to deny it, and that's when we went the legal route, which ended up taking 4.5 years to resolve.

## The lowest point

After the lawsuit was filed, they stopped paying me entirely. I was using the revenue from my buyout to fund my own business-to-business wireless company, so the revenue I was depending on to pay staff and rent for our office and product completely disappeared. I ended up laying off staff and breaking my lease, which caused another lawsuit, and endured some very difficult financial times.

Added by Brett on review, 2026-11-02: the lowest-point moment was when he looked at the bank account, realized they did not have enough money to pay the overhead, and had to let people go. "I felt like a failure." (Dictated as "didn't realize we had enough money"; meaning confirmed by context.) Brett also confirmed this partnership is the 2000 wireless business in brett-profile.md (one location to eight in about 18 months).

## The toll

Economically, the lawsuit ended up costing me somewhere between $750,000 and $1 million that I never recovered. More importantly, it took an incredible emotional toll because I was so angry that I allowed it to consume me. All I wanted to do was win and get revenge. Carrying around so much anger for that period of time made it virtually impossible for me to move forward with my life and build a successful business. Only after the lawsuit ended and the company went bankrupt was I able to look back and truly understand how much more it cost me than what I was owed financially.

## What he pulled forward

1. The importance of contracts in business. We were close friends, so I took this for granted and never finalized my partnership agreement. Their legal position was that they were trying to prove that I was never actually a partner, which ultimately we overcame. Every single contingency needs to be well thought out and documented in an operating agreement.
2. Avoid lawsuits and litigation at all costs. They are incredibly expensive financially and emotionally.
3. Partnerships are like marriages. If you rush into them too quickly, there's a high chance they're not going to end well. The most important thing is compatibility and complementary skill sets. You need to each bring something of significant value to the table. Equity is based on contribution.
