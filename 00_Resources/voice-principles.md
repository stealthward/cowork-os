# Voice Principles – Brett Ward

Read this file before writing any content on Brett's behalf.

---

## Overall Voice

Write like a smart peer over coffee, not a lecturer. Conversational, confident, and no-nonsense. Talk *to* people, not at them. Blend business clarity with human warmth.

---

## How Brett actually writes (read before drafting)

Drawn from his REACH draft, scrubbed against the rules below. Match these before applying any rule. (Brett, 2026-10-13)

- Match the point of view to the message and the voice; "I," "we," and "you" are interchangeable when the piece calls for it. Brett leans toward "we" because it reads bigger and keeps the focus off him, and uses "I" for a decision, a view, or a lesson that is his alone. Teaching pieces (REACH, how-tos) usually talk to the reader as "you," with Brett's story as a short aside. Operator lessons and story posts usually stay in "we" or "I" so the reader learns by watching, but a line to the reader is fine when it serves the point. Mixing them is never a fix by itself; a switch that confuses who did what is. (Brett, 2026-10-13; loosened 2026-10-28)
- Always use contractions: you're, don't, it's, I've.
- Build it this way: open on a scene, question, or claim the reader recognizes; name their problem; back it with one stat or an everyday comparison (Nike, Geico, a pond and an ocean); give a plain definition or a simple model; close by naming the lesson in one or two lines whenever possible. A question and its answer works ("The biggest lesson? ..."). (Brett, 2026-10-13)
- Define terms plainly in his own words: "I define good content as..."
- Take a stance when the usual industry answer is wrong: "I disagree."
- His words: simple, think of you first, top of mind, by far, That's it.
- Say "we" instead of "I" whenever the work was a team effort. It reads bigger and keeps the focus off Brett. Keep "I" for a decision or a view that is his alone. (Brett, 2026-10-13)
- Tell it as a story with feeling: open on the moment, show the stakes (what was about to break, what could be lost), then the hard decision and what it cost, then where things stand now. (Brett, 2026-10-13)

Example 1, personal aside:
> That's not to say selling real estate is easy, but I do consider it simple. And that's a good thing. As someone with a history of complicating things, I crave simplicity, especially in business. That's because simple is much easier to scale. Find out what works, and just do more of it.

Example 2, scene, proof, landing line:
> "Hey Allison… how have you been? I haven't seen you at drop-off this year."
> "Cheryl, so good to see you, things are great! We lucked out, found our dream home! Sold our place in 2 weeks and got way over asking!"
> (Awkward silence)
> "Oh shoot! I totally forgot you were in real estate... We would have called you."
>
> I've asked that question to hundreds of agents, and most with more than five years in the business say "Yes," often followed by "...more times than I can remember." That's because memory is your worst enemy. It can cost you tens of thousands of dollars every time someone forgets that you sell real estate. [...] Statistically, 2 out of every 3 listings come from referrals and past clients. And 8 out of 10 sellers hire the first agent they talk to. That means your biggest opportunity to grow, by far, comes from people who already know you, as long as you reach them first. [...] That's it. The more people who think of you first, the more homes you'll sell. Period.

Example 3, stance:
> The term "SOI" gets thrown around a lot in real estate training circles. In my experience, most definitions are way too broad to be useful. You'll frequently hear things like: "Your SOI is everyone you know who knows you by name," "all the people you would stop and speak to in public," or "every contact in your phone." I disagree. Knowing someone's name is not influence. Saying hello in public or having their phone number is not influence either. Influence is the ability to shape decisions and change behavior in others.

Example 4, problem framing:
> Most agents create content. Very few create good content. I define good content as "interesting and relevant to the people who see it." That's it. Scroll through most agents' feeds and you'll see the same thing: listings, market updates, sales posts, and open house announcements. All real estate, all the time. [...] Unless someone is buying, selling, or thinking about it, they don't care. [...] Every time you post something people don't care about, a few more stop paying attention.

Example 5, decision and outcome (Brett's own rewrite, 2026-10-13):
> We reached a point where our growth outpaced our team's capacity, so we made the difficult decision to halt all business development and focus exclusively on strengthening operations and expanding the team. Turning away potential revenue is never easy, but it was the right decision. Today, we have the foundation to support our largest clients and the capacity to take on new ones. More importantly, we've learned how to scale operations alongside revenue growth. With that foundation in place, I can shift my focus back to driving new business.

## Tone

- **Direct but friendly.** Get to the point fast, but never sound harsh. Clear about stakes and outcomes without drama or hype.
- **Casual-professional balance.** Relaxed openers are fine even in serious business conversations.
- **Supportive and reassuring.** Acknowledge frustration, then pivot to solutions and good news.

---

## Sentence Structure

- Write in complete sentences, casual or formal. Two exceptions: the landing line, and the text after a label in a labeled list ("Example: A move-up family in a top school district..."). Short and direct is fine. Grammatically incomplete is not.
- Every sentence needs a subject and verb. "Holiday season without a plan." is a fragment, not a sentence. Do not use it for emphasis.
- One exception, the landing line: a one-to-three-word sentence ("Simple." "That's it." "Period.") may close a point, once or twice per piece, never two in a row and never part of a staccato run. (Brett, 2026-10-13)
- Vary sentence length for rhythm, but never drop the verb for dramatic effect.
- Avoid staccato rhythm: three or more consecutive short sentences (under seven words each) read as performative. Consolidate them.
- Isolate a key sentence as its own paragraph to drive a point home, but that sentence must be complete.
- Use logical stepwise flows: problem, reason, solution. Or: define, identify, associate.

---

## Words and Phrases to Avoid

- Never use: "dive into," "game-changing," "straightforward," "leverage," "synergize," "circle back," "touch base"
- No em dashes. Use commas, colons, or periods instead.
- No stiff corporate language. If it sounds like a memo, rewrite it.
- Don't start sentences with "I think" or "I believe" when stating something directly.
- Avoid false contrast constructions: "It's not X, it's Y." These read as cliched and AI-generated.
- No fragment sentences used for punchy effect. "Less than three trips to Starbucks." has no verb. Rewrite it as a complete thought. The landing line and labeled list items in Sentence Structure are the only exceptions.
- No staccato sequences: "You saw it land. You spent it down." Three clipped sentences in a row reads like financial coaching content, not Brett's natural voice.
- If it sounds like it came from a language model, rewrite it.
- No opener patterns: "In today's [adjective] world/landscape," "In a world where," "Now more than ever," "Whether you're X or Y"
- No filler phrases: "It's worth noting that," "It's important to remember that," "Simply put," "At the end of the day," "Without further ado," "It goes without saying"
- No overused power words: "unlock," "elevate," "empower," "transform," "seamlessly," "robust," "cutting-edge," "ecosystem"
- No alliteration clusters: "proven, practical, and powerful" style constructions
- No fake dramatic pauses: "And here's the thing." used as a standalone theatrical line
- No weak closers: "What are your thoughts?" / "Let me know in the comments" / "I hope this helps!"

## AI Tells (added 2026-09-17 from the anti-AI writing comparison)

These are patterns that mark text as machine-written. Most were already covered above; these were not. Treat them as hard rules.

- No rule of three by reflex. Three adjectives, three phrases, three bullets every time reads as filler. Use two, or four, or the one that matters.
- No false ranges. "From first-time buyers to seasoned investors" means nothing unless the middle ground is real. Name the one audience you mean.
- No forced synonyms. Say "the agent" again instead of "the professional," "the practitioner," "the key player." Repeating the right word beats variety.
- No fake-depth participles. Cut "highlighting the importance of," "underscoring," "reflecting broader trends." If the point matters, give it its own sentence.
- Say "is" and "has." Not "serves as," "stands as," "represents," "boasts," "features."
- No significance inflation. State the fact and let the reader judge whether it is a turning point.
- No meta commentary. Do not announce what the paragraph is about to do.
- No dead transitions: "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Moreover," "That said," "With that in mind."
- No engagement bait: "Let that sink in," "Read that again," "This changes everything."
- No chat leakage in published writing: "Great question," "I hope this helps," "Would you like me to."
- Sentence case in headers, not Title Case.
- Vary the rhythm. Even-length sentences in even-length paragraphs read as a metronome. This does not license fragments; every sentence still carries a verb.

## Word Choice

- Plain language over jargon. Explain concepts simply.
- Conversational phrases over formal ones.
- Concrete, specific examples. References to real places, real scenarios, real agent experiences.
- Prefer "business development" or "client acquisition" over "sales" and "selling." Use "sales" only when it is the clearest word for the point. (Brett, 2026-10-13)
- Do not name specific tools or the tech stack (Asana, Zoho, and the like) unless the piece is about how Brett works in that platform. (Brett, 2026-10-13)

## Personality on the Page

- **Pragmatic and strategic.** Focus on what actually works.
- **Respectfully confident.** State opinions as clear truths without chest-thumping.
- **Relationship-oriented.** Emphasize partnership and mutual value.

---

## Common Patterns

- **Rhetorical questions.** Lead the reader to agree before teaching.
- **Mini-scenarios.** Short scenes create emotional hooks before the lesson or ask.
- **Clear CTAs when the piece has an ask** (company content, lead magnets, or when Brett asks for one). End with specific next steps. A give-first post closes on the lesson instead. (Brett, 2026-10-13)

---

## Formatting Defaults

- Bold key points for scannability: the turn of a story, the bridge into the steps, step names and labels, and the closing line. Keep the bold in the draft even when the platform doesn't render markdown; Brett formats it at posting. (Brett, 2026-10-19)
- One idea per paragraph.
- Use bullet points for lists, prose for explanations.
- Short paragraphs in emails and written content (1-3 sentences max).

---

## What Makes This Voice Distinct

- **Agent-centric framing.** Anchor advice in the lived reality of agents: listings, referrals, visibility, competition.
- **"Simple, not easy" honesty.** Strip away mystique. Branding isn't magic, it's consistent presence.
- **Tight, memorable lines.** Write quotable statements that read like pull-quotes.
- **Educator and operator.** Sounds like someone who teaches the playbook and runs the plays. Grounded in practice, not theory.

## Emojis and hashtags

No emojis in Brett's voice. No hashtags on any social post. (Brett, 2026-09-21 and 2026-09-23.)
