## 3. Standards every piece is judged against

### 3.1 Guide and Hero
The business is the guide. The client is the hero. The audience's problem, journey, and success is the center of the story; the business earns trust by showing it can help the hero win.
- The client's transformation is the story, not the transaction.
- Brett's accomplishments exist to validate his ability to guide; they are not the point.
- RTM and Five Star are trusted advisors, not service vendors.
- Content that leads with "we" or "I" and stays there is doing it wrong, except an operator-lesson post, which is told in "we" or "I" by design (section 2 map). (Brett, 2026-10-13)

### 3.2 REACH: the one goal
Build an audience of people who think of you first when they, or someone they know, is ready to buy or sell. Platforms, formats, and frequency serve this.

### 3.3 Good content, defined
Content is good when it is interesting and relevant to the people who see it. Good content speaks to people who are not in the market yet, so you are already trusted when the moment arrives. The audience decides if it is good; the test is whether the audience grows.

### 3.4 Brand vs. branding
Brand is what people associate with you. Branding is how you shape that perception through marketing, messaging, and behavior.

### 3.5 Quality and quantity
Both matter. Volume creates the feedback loop that shows what lands; quality earns and keeps attention. Produce at speed, learn from what lands, do more of that.

### 3.6 Give until they ask
Content deposits goodwill; asks withdraw it. Mature platforms run at about 3.5 to 1 (TV) and 4 to 1 (Facebook) give to ask; growing platforms give far more, so model those. Give in public, ask in private; the people who reach out self-select and are the best customers. Asking for money is the moment you choose to slow growth. ($100M Leads, "Mastering the Give : Ask ratio.")
- Applies to Brett's own content now. Company content (RTM, Five Star) keeps its calls to action.
- Editor checks: a value piece carries no ask; any batch stays far above 4 to 1.

## 4. The Content Unit: Hook, Retain, Reward

Source: $100M Leads, "#2 Post Free Content," Part I; Brett's Hook Retain Reward Doc (1D8onnrzlX6Ksi-VaEIKBuPQ9mREqGvVQqfgRWsD09Ys).

### 4.1 The unit
A content unit is the smallest piece that hooks, retains, and rewards attention.
1. **Hook attention:** get them to notice it. Three levers: topics, headlines, format.
2. **Retain attention:** get them to consume it.
3. **Reward attention:** satisfy the reason they started.
- Editor checks: hook, retention device, and payoff are all present; the payoff is the one the hook promised.

### 4.2 Hook lever 1: Topics
Personal experience first: no one else has lived Brett's life. Name the category in the brief.
1. **Far Past:** important past lessons, tied to what he does now ("the story without the scar").
2. **Recent Past:** something he did, then what happened. Mine last week's calendar, meetings, and conversations. Client stories live here.
3. **Present:** capture ideas the moment they come; if one beats normal, make more on that topic.
4. **Trending:** go where attention is and apply his experience to it. Widest reach.
5. **Manufactured:** pick a topic, learn it or do it on purpose, then show it. Most effort, biggest possible payoff.
6. **Other people's experience:** stories from clients, guests, partners, or the market.
- Editor checks: the topic is something only Brett could say.

### 4.3 Hook lever 2: Headlines
A headline gives the reason to consume and lets the viewer weigh the reward against everything else. Seven components; use at least two. The more it hits, the stronger it performs.
1. **Recency:** as recent as possible.
2. **Relevancy:** personally meaningful to the viewer.
3. **Celebrity:** prominent people or authorities, including a recognizable industry figure, or a credential such as a start date, a transaction count, or a dollar figure.
4. **Proximity:** close to home geographically.
5. **Conflict:** opposing ideas, people, or forces.
6. **Unusual:** odd, unique, rare, bizarre.
7. **Ongoing:** a story still unfolding, with updates or twists.
- Headline principles: curiosity, differentiation, appeal. A headline is new wrapping on the same idea.
- The first three seconds count visually and verbally. Lead with the most interesting version of the point; do not warm up to it.
- Thumbnails: the visual entry point; they communicate the topic before anyone reads a word.
- Editor checks: at least two components; the body pays off what the headline promises.

### 4.3a Hooks: how the opening is built (Brett, 2026-10-28)
The seven components are the ingredients; these are the principles every opening is built on, for every kind of content. They're platform-agnostic: each platform puts them in its own context. Apply the core to every piece, then the lane the piece belongs to, then the platform block.

**Core: every piece, every platform**
1. **Call Out and value.** Every hook has two parts. The Call Out earns the next second: the right reader thinks "that's me" or "what does that mean?" The value tells them what they get for staying: a specific, believable outcome about them, not about us. A strong value is concrete, relevant to the reader's current problem, worth the attention, easy to understand, and tied to at least one lever: speed, certainty, money, status, or reduced effort. When the Call Out raises a question, the next line answers it.
2. **First line, first second.** The first sentence spoken or read is the hook. It stands alone with no setup. Never open slow: no greeting, logo, intro, or credential before the point. A credential can carry the Celebrity component inside the line but never opens it.
3. **One pattern, named.** Build the hook on one of these and name it in the footer (Hormozi's names; lane limits below). Each template is a starting shape, filled only with real specifics:
   1. Statements: a flat, specific claim. Template: "The [fastest/easiest/most overlooked] way to [result] is [mechanism]."
   2. Narratives: a story opening mid-scene, outcome withheld. Template: "[Character] [unexpected event], and [unfinished consequence]."
   3. Provocative claims: a line against a belief the reader holds, or a surprising claim with an unexpected reason. Template: "[Belief the reader holds] is [wrong or backwards] because [unexpected reason]."
   4. Commands: tell the reader to do or stop something ("Stop..." counts here). Template: "[Do or stop] [action] if you want [result]."
   5. Lists: a count of items. The body delivers every item (4.6). Template: "[Number] [ways, mistakes, or steps] to [result] or avoid [pain]."
   6. Conditionals and If-Then Statements: the reader's situation, then its consequence. Template: "If you [situation], then [consequence, insight, or action]."
   7. Ridiculous Results: a specific, out-of-the-ordinary result. The body proves it. Template: "How [who] got [specific result] in [timeframe] without [expected sacrifice]."
   8. Questions: Yes-Questions, which name a specific situation so the reader answers "yes, that's me," or open questions that create curiosity. Template: "Do you [painful situation] and want [result]?"
   9. Labels: words that name the audience directly ("Escrow assistants ready to become officers,"). Template: "[Specific audience], [specific payoff]."
   10. Admission: a mistake or loss stated plainly. Template: "I [mistake or loss], and [what it cost or taught]."
4. **Two components.** It carries at least two of the seven (4.3).
5. **Awareness.** Name the reader's awareness level and sell what fits it. Unaware: an unexpected consequence. Problem Aware: the pain and a possible cause. Solution Aware: the specific mechanism. Product Aware: the product, a concrete outcome, low effort. Most Aware: the offer, proof, urgency, scarcity, or risk reversal. One hook for every audience is the core mistake.
6. **Meat.** The hook sets an expectation and the body proves exactly that. Pick the meat with the hook: Testimonial (someone like me got it), Demonstration (the mechanism works), Education (now I understand the problem), Story (I need to know what happened).
7. **Verbal and nonverbal.** On anything visual, the picture stops the scroll and the words give it direction; both say one thing. Nonverbal hooks: starting mid-action, a dramatic before-and-after, on-screen text that isolates the promise. A flashy visual with generic words gets attention without relevance.
8. **Never:** a generic statement; a meta announcement ("here's how," "this is what we do"); a vague setup; a generic question; a private metaphor that needs explaining; a value that is vague ("improve your business"), generic, overhyped, or about us; curiosity the body never pays off; an invented number, client, or result.
9. **Options.** When the copywriter offers hooks, it writes three to five and gives a table: the hook, its pattern, the awareness level, the meat, the components it carries, and, where a platform has a fold, its character count. Once testing applies, the table adds a source column: winner, permutation, or new.

**Lanes: by kind of content**
10. **Brett's own content (give-first).** The Call Out is a situation or claim the reader recognizes, never a Label. No call to action (3.6). Questions open only as a Yes-Question, never by default. Awareness stays at Unaware, Problem Aware, or Solution Aware. Point of view ("I," "we," or "you") follows the message (voice-principles.md), so any pattern this lane allows fits any piece type. Testimonial meat needs a documented client story.
11. **Company content (RTM, Five Star).** Every pattern is open, Labels included. All five awareness levels; Product Aware and Most Aware are for readers who already know the company. A call to action is allowed (3.6). Testimonials only from real, documented clients. Five Star copy never implies a referral payment, gift, or exchange of business for referrals (RESPA Section 8); it leads with certainty, communication, speed, and client experience, and is flagged for counsel before launch. Replace adjectives (smoother, clearer) with the mechanism the company actually commits to.
12. **Ads and offers with a strong call to action.** Structure: Call Out + Value + CTA ($100M Leads). Keep the Call Out (the first few seconds) and the CTA the same across long and short versions. Build the value with What-Who-When as $100M Leads defines it: What, the eight key elements of the value equation; Who, whose status changes; When, the experience across past, present, and future. An offer-led headline combines specificity, outcome, timeframe, audience, and perceived value ($100M Offers). Pick one awareness level per ad.

**Platforms**
13. **LinkedIn text.** The first sentence lands inside about 140 characters (the mobile "see more" fold; an observed estimate, LinkedIn publishes none); aim for 10 words or fewer. When the value isn't in the first sentence, the second line delivers it, and both sit above the fold. Question openers measure lowest here. Phone test before posting: read only what shows above "see more." Would a top producer stop?
14. **Short-form video (reels, Shorts, TikTok, LinkedIn native video).** The first three seconds carry the hook, visually and verbally: the nonverbal hook stops the scroll, the verbal hook names the reader and the payoff, then every sentence sells the next second. The post's first line, the thumbnail, and the first three seconds say one thing. The video works muted, with captions.
15. **Adding a platform.** Hook best practice is set platform by platform as each one is added. When Brett adds a platform, the CMO sources that platform's hook practice (4.4), and Brett approves a block here that puts the core and the lanes in that platform's context. Until a platform has its block, apply the core and the lane only. (Brett, 2026-10-28)

**Swipe file and testing**
16. **Swipe file (starts now; it uses other people's results, not ours).** Collect the 50 best hooks from top-performing content in the market, the hook only (opening line, visual, first promise). Tag each by audience, platform, format, awareness level, meat, and performance. Swipes model the hook; they are never copied word for word (4.4). The LinkedIn swipe file lives at Content & Authority/Content & Authority Resources/linkedin-hook-swipe-file.md; the copywriter reads it before writing LinkedIn hook options. (Brett, 2026-10-29)

Items 17 onward apply once a lane has performance data of its own.
17. **Winners and 70/20/10.** A winner beats comparable hooks with the same audience, offer, platform, format, and time period, judged against our own account, never an invented threshold. Read three tiers: attention (thumb-stop, hold rate), consumption (watch time, completion), business (qualified leads, booked calls). An attention winner with no business result is not a winner. Each new batch is 70% proven winners, 20% permutations, and 10% new. A permutation changes one variable (audience, problem, mechanism, format, or specificity) and keeps the promise.
- Editor checks: the hook names its lane, pattern, awareness level, and meat; the Call Out and value are both present, the value is about the reader and pulls at least one lever (speed, certainty, money, status, reduced effort); it carries two components; it fits its lane's limits; it breaks none of the bans; the platform block is met (the LinkedIn fold, the first three seconds on video); the body delivers the meat the hook promised.
- Sources: Operations/AI Workforce/drafts/2026-10-28-linkedin-opening-research.md (vendor studies, directional); 2026-10-28 Hook Writing Training Brief, Acquisition.com AI (Google Doc 1luCeUF8UEx1o97HlsYevIt5PUYtFclPD78DpIDW3tfk; drawn from $100M Playbook: Hooks, $100M Playbook: GOATed Ads, $100M Leads, and $100M Offers; page references unverified; Call Out types and What-Who-When checked against drafts/hormozi-extraction/leads-paid-ads.md).

### 4.4 Hook lever 3: Format
People consume what looks like things they already liked.
1. Format for the platform first.
2. Tweak for the ideal audience.
3. Model the best-performing content in his market on that platform.
- Source material (REACH, transcripts, docs) supplies the ideas, not the copy. Rewrite each concept in the platform's native style. A reader of both should recognize the concept without thinking it was copy-pasted. Individual sentences or short passages may stay verbatim; a whole paragraph or most of the piece tracking the source's wording is a fix. (Brett, 2026-10-19)
- Editor checks: looks native to the platform; when a CMO brief is supplied, it names a benchmark piece. Without a brief, "Benchmark: none supplied" passes. (Brett, 2026-10-13)

### 4.5 Retain: Lists, Steps, Stories
Curiosity is the main driver. Plant an unanswered question, stated or implied.
1. **Lists:** items on a theme in any order. State the number in the headline or first seconds.
2. **Steps:** actions in a required order toward a result. Clear, valuable early steps make people want the rest.
3. **Stories:** events, usually with a lesson; they run on "what happens next."
- In written posts, each step runs its name, then three labeled parts in the same order every time: "How to do it:", "Why it's important:", "Example:". A step missing a part is a fix. (Brett, 2026-10-19) In direct-to-camera scripts, each step still covers the how, the why, and an example, but spoken conversationally with no labels, varied sentence patterns, and a bridge into the next beat. Spoken labels or a repeated step template are a fix. (Brett, 2026-10-26)
- Steps are less flexible with a clearer reward; lists are flexible with a less explicit reward. All three combine.
- Build the story out, don't just transcribe it. Draw the logical outcomes of what Brett said (halting business development means turning away revenue and clients) and use his beliefs on file (REACH, brett-profile, growth-strategy) to explain why he took a position. Never invent specifics: numbers, names, dates, dollar figures, or quotes come only from the source, his profile, or his interview answers. Mark each inference "inferred" in the source map. (Brett, 2026-10-13) Use as few inferred lines as the turn needs; one tight line that implies the cost beats several that spell it out. (Brett, 2026-10-19)
- Editor checks: an open question is planted and closed; anything called "steps" is truly sequential; each inference follows logically and matches Brett's beliefs on file; an invented specific is a fix.

### 4.6 Reward: value per second
Quality is how often the piece rewards the viewer for the time it takes. Nothing is too long, only too boring.
- Failure modes: fewer items than promised, weak or already-heard items, the wrong audience.
- Editor checks: every promised item is delivered; every question opened is answered.

### 4.7 Short versus long form
Long content is content units linked together; each section needs its own hook and payoff. Start short, then build.
- Editor checks: no stretch of a long piece goes without a payoff.

## 5. 5P: Proof, Pain, Promise, Plan, Picture

Source: Brett's 5P Doc (14imr-5F8J4D9uJEmC4inV5O040wZ_siNyV524iODJas).

5P is a set of concepts woven into a piece, not a fixed sequence and not a headline formula. Any order works. The more Ps a piece carries clearly, the better; none is required.
- **Proof:** earns the right to speak.
- **Pain:** names the problem.
- **Promise:** says what they get.
- **Plan:** shows how.
- **Picture:** lets them see the result.

Brett's example, in his order: Pain (real estate content is difficult and time-consuming), Promise (in the next 60 seconds I'll show you how to make it easy), Proof (we've done more than 10K real estate videos), Plan (take this short evaluation and get a free content roadmap with scripts for your next 20 videos), Picture (all the extra time to close new listings).
- On Brett's own give-first content, the Plan is the value itself, not an offer.
- Editor checks: name the Ps present; a piece meant to convert carries a problem and an answer to it.

## 6. Direct-to-camera outline: hook, point, proof, close

1. **Topic:** a REACH principle, a business-building insight, or a timely observation (source category from 4.2).
2. **Hook:** the headline spoken in the first three seconds (4.3).
3. **Point:** one claim, retained with a list, steps, or a story (4.5).
4. **Proof:** Brett's own experience, told as "How I" (5, Proof; 8.1).
5. **Close:** pay off the hook in one sentence the viewer can repeat (4.6). No ask unless it is company content.
Then script or talking points, shoot, distribute.

## 7. Copywriting rules

Source: "Hormozi copywriting advice" Doc (14 Years of Marketing Advice in 35 Minutes).
1. **Say what only you can say:** unique, verifiable proof over vague promises.
2. **Call out who it is for, and who it is not:** be specific, match the reader's awareness, describe real moments of frustration.
3. **Always have a reason why:** "because" raises compliance; each line sells the next line.
4. **Damaging admissions:** admit the flaw first. "[damaging truth] BUT [what you want them to believe]."
5. **Show, don't tell:** describe the moment, not the benefit.
6. **Implied authority:** let experience, clientele, and volume answer "why believe you" without claiming it.
7. **Third grade reading level:** simple beats clever; assume a distracted reader.
8. **Humor:** a differentiator, not a crutch; test it.
9. **Layout (LinkedIn):** one or two sentences per paragraph, with white space between paragraphs; numbered steps or a list whenever the reader needs something actionable. (ACQ.ai LinkedIn brief; Brett, 2026-10-29)
10. **One idea per post:** a second idea is a second post. One idea can run in several formats (reel caption, story post, list); change the format, not the message. (ACQ.ai LinkedIn brief; Brett, 2026-10-29)
- Editor checks: one line on each, folded into the voice checklist.

## 8. Lessons from making content

Source: $100M Leads, "7 Lessons I've Learned From Making Content." Six load; "avoid pre-scheduling" is left out (Brett, 2026-10-05: scheduled publishing stays the default).
1. **Teach like REACH, prove with "How I."** Talk to the reader and give them the model and the steps. Brett's own experience is the proof, what he did and prefers, which cannot be argued with. No preachy commands ("you need to," "you must") and no promised results. (Brett, 2026-10-13)
2. **We need to be reminded more than we need to be taught.** Repeat core points; he will tire of a message long before the audience has seen it.
3. **Puddles, Ponds, Lakes, Oceans.** Start with a narrow niche plus place, then widen.
4. **Content creates tools for salespeople.** Keep a "greatest hits" list tagged with the problem each solves; send them before or after conversations.
5. **Free content retains paying customers.** Bad free content lowers clients' view of the paid work.
6. **People don't have shorter attention spans, they have higher standards.**
- Editor checks: teaches the reader and proves it with Brett's experience; no preachy commands or promised results; scoped to Brett's niche.

## 10. Planning

### 10.1 Benchmarks
Monthly: total followers and reach (size); rate of growth, new divided by starting (speed). Only inputs are controllable: fix a posting cadence and an ask cadence per platform and do not stop. Used in the CMO's monthly report on initiative 4.

### 10.2 Scaling
- **Depth then width:** post on one platform, regularly; maximize quality and quantity there; add a platform while holding the first; repeat. Fewer resources, compounds; relies on one channel early.
- **Width then depth:** post on one platform, regularly; add the next while holding the last until on all relevant platforms; maximize all together. Broader reach faster, allows repurposing; more labor.
- Every content plan names which one it follows.

## 11. What Brett teaches agents (topics, not structures)

Brett's audience is real estate agents, so the frameworks agents use are subject matter for his content: a post or script can teach the Three Spheres, the SOI pillars, or the transaction run. They are not structures for Brett's own posts (he does not run his own deals as a transaction run). When a piece teaches one, it still follows the map in section 2. (Brett, 2026-10-06.)

### 11.1 The Three Spheres (RTM client framework)
- **Sphere of Influence (SOI):** people who think of the agent first, would hire them, and would refer others. Protect with consistent, authentic content.
- **Sphere of Recognition (SOR):** people who know the agent but do not associate them with real estate first. Build association through lifestyle, community, and interest-based content.
- **Sphere of Proximity (SOP):** people in the market who do not know the agent. Build relationships with community influencers and organizations through collaborative, story-driven content.
- **The binary strategy:** every piece either protects the SOI or expands it.

### 11.2 SOI content pillars (agent reference)
Personal life snapshots; soft professional proof (client stories, transaction lessons, milestones); helpful homeowner tips; gratitude and recognition.

### 11.3 Turn a Transaction into a Content Run
1. The story: who you helped and why it mattered.
2. The process: a behind-the-scenes moment or problem you solved.
3. The result: connect the outcome to what the client cared about.
4. The education: one lesson that helps a future buyer or seller.

### 11.4 The 75/25 content ratio (RTM client framework)
Source: Content Authority & Social Media Playbook, section 6. The default balance for an agent's feed, changed only when the business model justifies it.
- 75 to 80 percent audience-building content: lifestyle, community, shared interests, local features, homeowner education, local interviews, local business and event spotlights.
- 20 to 25 percent professional real estate content: listings, case studies, negotiation wins, market analysis, process breakdowns, client results, seller and buyer guidance.
- Objective: earn attention first, reinforce expertise second. Too much real estate content repels people who aren't in the market; too little weakens authority.
