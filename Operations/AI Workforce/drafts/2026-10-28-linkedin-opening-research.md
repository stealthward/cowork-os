# LinkedIn post openings for a text-plus-video post: research scope (2026-10-28)

Prepared for Brett Ward via the CMO. Web research only. No contact made, no Zoho writes, no paid lookups. Read-only scope: inputs 1 to 8 in the sidekick file were not needed for a pure method question.

## Summary

LinkedIn does not publish a "see more" number, so every figure is an observed estimate from third parties: plan on about 140 characters on mobile (range 90 to 150, one source says up to 230) and about 210 on desktop for a post with video attached (range 210 to 250), with line breaks counting toward the cut. That makes the first sentence the whole hook: one specific claim, ideally under about 10 words, that makes sense with no setup. The better-supported patterns are a flat specific claim, a story that opens mid-scene and withholds the outcome, a contrarian line, and a number tied to a result; the weakest are generic announcements, "excited to share" openers, vague setups, and generic questions. LinkedIn's own help center describes no video title field, only an optional text box, a thumbnail, and captions, so for a native video the post's first line is the headline and the thumbnail is the visual title. For a video post, the first line, the thumbnail, and the first three seconds of the video should say one consistent thing, and the video must work with sound off. Almost all the data is vendor-produced (AuthoredUp, MagicPost, Socialinsider, Taplio), so treat it as directional.

| [Lindsey Gamble, quoting LinkedIn's Julia Flavin](https://www.lindseygamble.com/blog/linkedins-senior-director-of-product-shares-key-tips-for-video-during-ongoing-tests) | "only 100 characters are visible in full-screen" | n/a | March 26 (year not shown on page; could not confirm) | Closest to LinkedIn itself, but refers to the full-screen vertical video player, not the main feed |

What this means:
- Treat 140 characters as the safe budget for the first sentence. Everything past it is hidden on most phones.
- With video attached, desktop shows fewer lines (about 3) than a text-only post (about 5), per the one source that separates them (Supergrow, vendor, single source).
- Line breaks count toward the cut, so a first line followed by blank lines uses up space. Put the claim in line one and let line two carry the turn.
- Only the 3,000-character post maximum is confirmed on LinkedIn's help pages (per eden.so). Brett's few hundred words are well inside it.
- Preview in the composer on a phone before posting; that is the only reliable check.

## 2. First line and first sentence

What the data says (all vendor studies, correlation only):
- Length: [MagicPost](https://magicpost.in/blog/linkedin-hooks), 1,179,958 posts, 12 months to June 2026, median likes: 1 to 5 words 30, 6 to 10 words 29, 11 to 15 words 25, 16+ words 28. The gap is small. Short wins slightly.
- Numbers: same study, first line with a digit 35 median likes vs 26 without.
- Questions: MagicPost says question openers earn 19 median likes vs 29 without (34% lower), holding in all three follower bands. [AuthoredUp](https://authoredup.com/blog/linkedin-hook-examples) (309,614 personal posts, Dec 2025 to May 2026, updated Aug 11, 2026) ranks Question lowest of five patterns at 2.16% median engagement vs Story 2.60%, Contrarian 2.31%, Statement 2.27%, Results 2.19%. A [dev.to analysis](https://dev.to/olams/i-analyzed-50000-linkedin-posts-heres-what-actually-gets-engagement-56l9) (50,247 posts, undated, keyword-regex classification) had question at 3.4%, mid-table. Two of three say questions underperform; the third is the weakest source.
- Worst openers (dev.to, weak method): generic announcement 0.8%, self-promotional 0.6%, vague statement 0.5%, link only 0.4%.

What practitioners say to avoid: claiming work you did not do, calling obvious things a "secret," trivial mistakes presented as major, contrarian for its own sake ([Tim Queen](https://timqueen.com/linkedin-hooks/), undated). Build the hook so the body pays off the promise (several vendor guides agree; none are primary evidence).

For Brett's audience: top-producing agents read for something usable. A flat specific claim or a number from his own escrow or media experience fits give-first. Leave out "here's what I learned" and any line that announces the post instead of saying the thing.

## 3. Titles and headlines

- Native video title: I found no LinkedIn documentation of a feed-visible title field. LinkedIn's [help page on posting video](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a7486279) lists only: an optional text box ("What do you want to talk about? (optional)"), a video thumbnail upload, and captions (auto-captions in 10 languages, or a caption file). The only source describing a title is a [Vista Social support article](https://support.vistasocial.com/hc/en-us/articles/16799786654363-How-to-add-video-titles-for-LinkedIn-posts) (undated; fetch returned 403, so I only have the search snippet), which says a title entered in the video's Settings "will appear on your LinkedIn post" at the bottom of the video. That describes a third-party scheduler. I cannot confirm LinkedIn's own composer shows it. One guide says the title defaults to the uploaded file name, so name the file sensibly in case it appears. Test on a draft before relying on it.
- If a title does show: write it as a plain statement of the benefit for the viewer, not a repeat of the first line, and keep it short. This is practitioner advice (one vendor blog), weak support.
- What actually works as the headline on a text-plus-video post: (1) the first line of the post text; (2) the video thumbnail, which LinkedIn lets you choose or upload (help page above; vendors advise a custom thumbnail with a few words on it because the auto frame can be weak); (3) burned-in captions on the first seconds.
- Other headline-like elements: a document or carousel has a title (LinkedIn's help center says AI may generate a carousel title where available, per search summary; not verified on the page), and an article or newsletter has a headline and, since June 2025, can use a video as its cover ([Social Media Today](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/linkedin-adds-video-header-images-articles-newsletters/751813/) via search, June 2025). These do not apply to a native video post.

## 4. Video-specific

- Autoplay and sound: sources say feed video autoplays muted and many views are silent. Estimates are 70% ([dev.to, Dec 20, 2025, unsourced](https://dev.to/synergistdigitalmedia/linkedins-video-algorithm-actually-rewards-this-not-what-everyone-says-50ik)), over 80%, and 85% (other vendor blogs, unsourced). The consistent advice is captions on every video, and burned-in captions over auto-generated. [OpusClip](https://www.opus.pro/blog/linkedin-video-hooks-formulas) (Nov 11, 2025) adds that the thumbnail and opening shot must stand out among text-heavy posts. The 3-second claim is repeated everywhere but no source gives a hold-rate number.
- LinkedIn's own words: Julia Flavin, LinkedIn Senior Director of Product Marketing, per Lindsey Gamble (year not confirmed): open with a strong hook; the Videos for You carousel shows the first three seconds; post copy helps LinkedIn understand the video's context; only 100 characters are visible in full-screen, so the opening text should be compelling enough to get people to watch and read on. This is the strongest LinkedIn-sourced guidance, though secondhand.
- So yes, the first line should agree with the thumbnail and first three seconds. The line makes the claim; the first caption on screen repeats or sharpens it. Do not make the video open with a different promise than the text.
- Video vs text-only (data):
  - [Socialinsider](https://www.socialinsider.io/blog/linkedin-content-study/) (2,450,610 brand posts, published Mar 16, 2026; brand pages, not personal profiles, and the page notes some 2025 values stand in for 2026): video 6.00%, text 4.50%, document 7.00%, multi-image 6.45%. A Q2 2026 summary via search put video at 5.90% vs text 3.95%.
  - [Taplio](https://taplio.com/linkedin-benchmarks/mar-26) (219,456 posts, March 2026): image, video, and document posts outperform text-only; its February report had image leading and text-only at 1.98%. Read via search summary only.
  - dev.to (undated): text 3.1%, video under 60s 2.8%, video over 60s 1.9%, which contradicts the others; weak method.
  - van der Blom's 2025 report, via a [secondary summary](https://www.writtenlyhub.com/news/linkedin-engagement-down-50-algorithm-insights-report-2025): native video up about 69%, especially vertical under 60 seconds; text-only down nearly 18%; views down about 50% overall. I could not get the 2026 report itself.
  - Counter-signal: [The State of Brand](https://thestateofbrand.com/news/linkedin-killing-video-reach) (May 5, 2026), citing Socialinsider, reports brand video views down 36% year over year.
  - A single-creator 90-day test ([Search Engine Journal](https://www.searchenginejournal.com/linkedin-video-posts-generate-3x-more-reach-study-finds/532073/), via search) found video reached 3x more people while text did better with existing connections. Anecdotal.
- Net: video usually beats text-only on engagement rate in 2026 data, documents beat video, and video reach is contested. A 60-second vertical reel with real text above it is consistent with every source, but none tests that exact combination.

## 5. LinkedIn's own guidance

- Help center, [posting a video](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a7486279): post text optional; thumbnail upload; captions. No guidance on openings or titles.
- LinkedIn product marketing (secondhand via Lindsey Gamble): see section 4. Vertical 9:16, leave headroom so the full-screen player does not crop to 2:3, strong hook, first three seconds, opening text.
- I did not find LinkedIn Marketing Solutions or creator-blog guidance on feed post openings or video titles. Most "best practice" hits for ads are third-party.

## Could not verify

- Any official LinkedIn "see more" cutoff number. None exists in what I read; all figures are observed estimates and disagree (mobile 90 to 230, desktop 210 to 700).
- Whether LinkedIn's own composer has a feed-visible native video title. Only a third-party scheduler's help article mentions one, and I could not read it (403).
- The 2026 Algorithm InSights report by Richard van der Blom. Only the 2025 report via a secondary summary, and the 2026 report's existence and scope from podcast listings. No hook or opening findings from his data were found. A page attributing a "hook in the first 3 lines" rule to him cites the 2025 report unclearly, so I did not use it.
- Buffer, Hootsuite, and Social Insider: no hook study found from any of them. Socialinsider's format benchmarks have no hook finding. I did not fetch Buffer's or Hootsuite's own LinkedIn pages.
- Hook examples from Justin Welsh, Lara Acosta, Chris Donnelly, or Jasmin Alic: searches returned none of their posts, so the examples above are from AuthoredUp's named featured posts (Collins, Hills, van Breugel, Broekema, Lakajev), which are real posts but not the largest accounts. Their post dates are not given.
- The year of the Lindsey Gamble article quoting LinkedIn's Julia Flavin (page shows March 26 only), and whether the "100 characters" limit applies to anything beyond the full-screen video player.
- The effect of video on the desktop fold (3 lines vs 5) rests on one tool vendor.
- Causation. Every study is correlational and vendor-run; the follower mix (personal vs brand pages) differs between studies. Socialinsider is brand pages, not personal profiles like Brett's.
- Taplio's monthly benchmarks and the Search Engine Journal single-creator test were read through search summaries only, not full pages.
- The 70%, 80%, and 85% sound-off figures have no primary source.
- Smartsheet, Zoho, Gmail, Asana, and the 00_Resources files were not read, because the question is a method question that touches no pipeline data.

## Decision for Brett

None needed. One practical test is worth doing before the first post: draft it in the LinkedIn composer, upload the reel, and check on a phone (a) where "see more" falls, and (b) whether the Settings menu on the video offers a title field.
