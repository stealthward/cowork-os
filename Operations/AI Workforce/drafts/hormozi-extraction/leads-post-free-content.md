### How Building an Audience Works (the free-content flywheel)
- **Name:** How Building An Audience Works - You Post Great Free Content
- **What it is for:** Explains why free content beats warm reach outs for engaged leads per hour invested, and how the audience compounds.
- **Parts or steps:**
  1. Post free content once; it reaches many people (warm reach outs require repeating yourself to each).
  2. People who find it valuable join your warm audience.
  3. If they think others will value it, they share it; those people may join too. The sharing can continue indefinitely.
  4. Once in a while, make an offer; if it has enough value, they take it and you make money.
- **Rules and numbers:**
  - Posting free content grows the warm audience; a constantly growing audience is made of people more likely to buy. The bigger the audience, the more money.
  - Free content makes all other advertising more effective (prospects who find no related content are less likely to buy).
  - Three trade-offs: harder to personalize so fewer respond; you compete with everyone else posting free content; if you stand out, people copy you, so constantly innovate.
  - Part I covers two topics: the content unit (hook, retain, reward) and linking units together; Part II covers monetizing.
  - Chapter subtitle: no one has complained about getting too much value (paraphrase).
- **Fits piece type:** content strategy and planning (judgment).
- **Copywriter uses it by:** framing a posting plan as one piece reaching many, with offers occasional.
- **Editor checks:** Does the plan treat the audience, not the single post, as the asset?
- **Source:** Part I, "How Building An Audience Works - You Post Great Free Content"

### The Content Unit (Hook, Retain, Reward)
- **Name:** The Content Unit - Three Components (Hook attention, Retain attention, Reward attention)
- **What it is for:** Explains that all audience-growing content does one thing: it rewards the person consuming it. The content unit is the smallest amount of material that hooks, retains and rewards attention.
- **Parts or steps:**
  1. Hook: get them to notice your content (give them a reason to redirect attention to you).
  2. Retain: get them to consume it.
  3. Reward: satisfy the reason they consumed it to begin with.
- **Rules and numbers:**
  - A person is rewarded only if they (1) have a reason to consume it, (2) pay attention long enough to (3) get that reason satisfied. The three components are those outcomes reversed.
  - A unit can be as small as an image, a meme or a sentence, so hook, retain and reward can all happen at once.
  - Hook effectiveness is "measured by the percentage of people who start consuming your content."
  - Attention is a competition: beat every alternative they have; make yourself the best option.
  - Hook has three levers that raise the percentage of people who pick your content: topics they find interesting, headlines that give them a reason, and a format matching other content they like (covered in the next three entries).
  - The author separates the three components only to discuss them; short tweets, memes or a jingle can go viral because they do all three at once.
  - Part I item 1 is titled "Hook"; it is the framing for Topics, Headlines and Format below.
- **Fits piece type:** all of them (judgment).
- **Copywriter uses it by:** checking every draft has a hook, a retention device and a payoff before writing the body.
- **Editor checks:** Does the piece hook, retain and reward? Is the promised reward actually delivered?
- **Source:** Part I, "The Content Unit - Three Components"

### Hook: Topics (five categories)
- **Name:** Topics: Far Past, Recent Past, Present, Trending, and Manufactured
- **What it is for:** Picking what the content is about so more people choose it. Author prefers personal experience because "there's only one of you" and no one else has lived your life.
- **Parts or steps:**
  1. Far Past: the important past lessons in your life; connect that wisdom to your product or service ("the story without the scar").
  2. Recent Past: do stuff, then talk about what you did or what happened. Mine last week's calendar, meetings, social interactions and warm reach out conversations. Testimonials and case studies fall in this category (a cool client story that gives value both promotes your services and helps the audience).
  3. Present: write down ideas at the exact time they come to you; always have a way to record in arm's reach. The author pauses meetings to note, text or email himself an idea; people do not mind, so that is not weird.
  4. Trending: go where the attention is; apply your own experience or expertise to what is trending.
  5. Manufactured: pick an interesting topic, then learn it, make it or do it, and show it to the world.
- **Rules and numbers:**
  - Far Past example structure is itself Hook / Retain / Reward (one-line version: hook, he complains of no time while on his phone; retain, a friend checks usage and finds three hours a day on social media; reward, the friend says they found him time). It works because it is relatable and ties his business (growing businesses) to a common struggle (not enough time).
  - Recent Past: take notes and recordings so it is easy to access; the result is a free, easy, valuable stash of content. Example: a tweet from a meeting with a portfolio CEO who copied the same offer as everyone else in his market and got subpar results (the tweet image was not in the text).
  - Present: post ideas publicly as they happen (the author tweets them); if a post does better than normal, make more on that topic.
  - Trending: very effective for gaining the attention of a broader audience.
  - Manufactured: costs the most time and effort but can have the biggest payouts (one-line example: living on $100 for a month).
  - Action Step: "Life happens - profit by sharing yours."
- **Fits piece type:** short-form video, LinkedIn post, long-form, topic planning (judgment).
- **Copywriter uses it by:** tagging each topic idea with one of the five categories and drawing from Brett's real calendar, deals and conversations first.
- **Editor checks:** Is the topic drawn from something only Brett could say? Is the category clear?
- **Source:** Part I, "1) Hook ... Topics"

### Hook: Headlines (seven components)
- **Name:** Headline components (from the news meta-analysis): Recency, Relevancy, Celebrity, Proximity, Conflict, Unusual, Ongoing
- **What it is for:** A headline is a short phrase or sentence that communicates the reason to consume; the audience uses it to weigh the likelihood of reward versus other options.
- **Parts or steps:**
  1. Recency: as recent as possible.
  2. Relevancy: personally meaningful to the viewer.
  3. Celebrity: including prominent people (celebrities, authorities).
  4. Proximity: close to home, geographically.
  5. Conflict: opposing ideas, people, nature, etc.
  6. Unusual: odd, unique, rare, bizarre.
  7. Ongoing: stories still in progress, evolving, with plot twists.
- **Rules and numbers:**
  - Source advises including at least two components in a headline.
  - Author prefers timeless principles over templates and draws them from the news, which he calls the best headline creator.
  - Source examples: Recency (an hour ago beats a year ago); Relevancy (nurses care more about nurse news); Celebrity (what Jeff Bezos eats for breakfast); Proximity (a neighbor's house fire vs one across the country); Conflict (pineapple on pizza, good vs evil, freedom vs security, justice vs mercy); Unusual (the six-fingered man at old circuses); Ongoing (updates every ten minutes when someone is in labor).
  - Action Step: include one or more components for meatier, attention-grabbing headlines.
- **Fits piece type:** short-form video hook, LinkedIn first line, email subject, ad headline (judgment).
- **Copywriter uses it by:** scoring each headline option against the seven and keeping versions with two or more (a local real estate angle fits Proximity and Relevancy, judgment).
- **Editor checks:** Does the headline contain at least two components? Does it promise a reward the body delivers?
- **Source:** Part I, "Headlines"

### Hook: Format
- **Name:** Format (match the platform)
- **What it is for:** Matching your content's look and feel to the best content on the platform so people recognize it as something that will reward them.
- **Parts or steps:**
  1. Format for the platform first.
  2. Tweak it so it hooks your ideal audience.
  3. Use the best content on the platform that targets your market as your guide.
- **Rules and numbers:** "People consume content because it's similar to stuff they've liked in the past." Formatting depends on both the audience and the platform. Bottom line: better-looking content will hook them before yours gets a chance if yours does not look like what they expect will reward them. Matching the popular format of the platform gets the most people to interact. The "Format example" shows four images with different looks and feels (images not in the text). Closing the Hook step, the author says always following these hook basics has put him in the top 1%.
- **Fits piece type:** short-form video, LinkedIn post, any platform-specific piece (judgment).
- **Copywriter uses it by:** pulling two or three top performers in the target niche on the platform and mirroring structure and look.
- **Editor checks:** Does it look native to the platform? Was a platform benchmark piece used?
- **Source:** Part I, "Format"

### Retain: Lists, Steps and Stories
- **Name:** Retain: Lists, Steps, Stories (embedded questions and curiosity)
- **What it is for:** Retaining attention by embedding unresolved questions in the audience's mind. Curiosity is the author's favorite driver of retention.
- **Parts or steps:**
  1. Lists: things, facts, tips, opinions, ideas presented one after the other, following a theme (e.g. "Top 10 Mistakes", "5 Biggest Money Makers"). State the number in the headline or first few seconds to set expectation; this retains more attention for longer.
  2. Steps: actions in order that accomplish a goal when completed. If early steps are clear and valuable, people want all of them.
  3. Stories: events, real or imaginary, usually with a lesson. Can be about things that happened, might happen, or will never happen; all drive "what happens next" curiosity.
- **Rules and numbers:**
  - Unresolved questions can be explicit (asked directly) or implicit (implied).
  - Curiosity is the favorite retention driver because people will wait years to learn what happens next (the author gets daily messages asking when his sales book is coming). His favorite way to create curiosity is embedding questions; the three favorite ways are lists, steps and stories.
  - Steps versus lists: steps happen in a required order to reach a result (less flexible, reward more explicit). Lists can hold nearly anything in any order (more flexible, reward less explicit).
  - Lists, steps and stories can be used alone or interwoven (lists within steps, a story per list item, stories reinforcing a step, a list of stories, many ongoing storylines). The chapter itself uses lists within steps with stories woven through.
  - Action Step: use lists, steps and stories to keep the audience curious; embed questions to make them want to know what happens next.
  - Author's list examples: "7 Ways I invested $1000 in my 20s That Paid Off Big"; "28 Ways To Stay Poor"; "A content unit has three pieces..." Steps examples: "3 Steps to Creating a Great Hook"; "How I Create a Headline in 7 Steps"; "The Morning Routine That Boosts My Productivity". Story examples: almost every chapter of the book has a story; "My editor made me do 19 drafts of this book - here's what I did to him"; a journey from sleeping on a gym's bottom floor to the top floor of a 5-star hotel.
  - Source text order is garbled in the extract for this section (the Lists definition appears before the Retain heading); the three definitions above are as the author gives them.
- **Fits piece type:** short-form video, LinkedIn carousel or post, long-form, email (judgment).
- **Copywriter uses it by:** choosing list, steps or story per piece and stating the count up front.
- **Editor checks:** Is there an open question the piece promises to close? Are steps truly sequential if labeled steps?
- **Source:** Part I, "2) Retain" (and the "Lists" paragraph preceding it)

### Reward
- **Name:** Reward (value per second)
- **What it is for:** Deciding whether content is actually good: how often it rewards the audience in the time it takes to consume it.
- **Parts or steps:**
  1. Hook the right audience with proper topics, headlines and formatting.
  2. Retain them with lists, steps and stories to get them curious and wanting more.
  3. Clearly satisfy the reason the content hooked them to begin with.
- **Rules and numbers:**
  - Key questions: Is it actually good? Does it satisfy the reason they watched? Does it make people want to share it? Quality is how often it rewards the audience in the time it takes to consume (value per second).
  - Too long is not the problem, too boring is: the same person bored at three seconds of a ten-second video may binge a 900-page book or eight hours of television.
  - You cannot guarantee a specific reward, only increase its chance.
  - Failure cases: the hook promises "7 Ways to Make Up with Your Spouse" and delivers (A) four ways, (B) seven weak or already-heard ways, or (C) goes to a room of single men; or the hook promises "4 Marketing Strategies Dentists Can Use" and dentists cannot use them. Result: people do not rewatch or share.
  - Rewarding means matching or exceeding expectations at the moment they chose to consume. The audience decides; the author has had content he expected to smash records that people skipped. Success test: the audience grows; if not, the content is not that good. Practice improves it.
  - Action Step: provide more value than anyone else, make good on promises, and completely answer the unresolved questions you embedded.
- **Fits piece type:** all (judgment).
- **Copywriter uses it by:** counting the promised items and delivering every one, with the strongest in the body.
- **Editor checks:** Does the piece deliver exactly what the hook promised? Does every question opened get answered?
- **Source:** Part I, "3) Reward"

### Short versus long form (linking units)
- **Name:** Short vs. long form: linking content units
- **What it is for:** Showing that long content is just content units linked together.
- **Parts or steps:** Not a numbered list. Single unit = hook, retain, reward once; long form = many units strung together.
- **Rules and numbers:** Shorter content hooks, retains and rewards fewer times; longer content does it more times and needs more skill because more "good" units in a row are required. Start with shorter versions even if you end up in long form. Example step: one step of a five-step list can be a unit; link all five for a longer piece. Analogies: a new comedian gets a few minutes on stage while only a master comic holds an hour; many epic-length novelists started with short stories. The passage points to a visual of linked units (not in the text). Once you can make a unit, the instruction is simply to make more, then grow the audience, then monetize (Part II).
- **Fits piece type:** long-form video, podcast, article (judgment).
- **Copywriter uses it by:** outlining long pieces as a chain of units, each with its own mini hook and payoff.
- **Editor checks:** Does each section reward the reader on its own? Is any stretch without a payoff?
- **Source:** Part I, closing passage that opens "So what's the difference between short and long form content?" (no heading in the text; the Recap calls it "Short vs. Long Form Content")

### Give : Ask ratio
- **Name:** Give : Ask ratio (and "Give until they ask")
- **What it is for:** Making offers without being a spam monster; deposit goodwill with content, withdraw it with offers.
- **Parts or steps:** Not stepped. Three reference points then the author's recommendation.
- **Rules and numbers:**
  - "Jab, jab, jab, right hook" (Gary Vaynerchuk) is the idea of giving many times before asking.
  - Goodwill model: content deposits goodwill, offers withdraw it; deposited goodwill raises attention and the chance they act on an ask, so the author tries to under-ask.
  - Television: 13 minutes of ads per 60 (47 giving, 13 asking), roughly 3.5:1 giving to asking.
  - Facebook: roughly 4 content posts for every 1 ad.
  - These are mature platforms that care more about monetizing than growing, so they show the minimum sustainable ratio; "give, give, give, ask" gets closer to maximum monetization without shrinking the audience. Most people want growth, so model growing platforms instead.
  - Growing platforms show lots of content and few ads: they dramatically over give and under ask. The more you reward the audience, the bigger it gets, so give far more than you ask.
  - Author's tweak: "Give until they ask." People are waiting for the ask; when you hold back they trust and share more. Receiving without giving back is uncomfortable, so they go to your site, DM or email to ask. You "give in public, ask in private"; they self-select; these are the best customers (even as payers they feel they got the better deal); growth never slows.
  - Bottom line: asking for money is the moment you decide to slow growth, so patience raises what you get when you finally ask.
  - Action Step: give until they ask.
  - Integrated example math (in the "Integrated" passage): 60-minute podcast with three 30-second ads = 58.5 min giving to 1.5 min asking, well above 3:1.
  - Warning (one line): a friend's podcast stopped growing and shrank after asking too often inside content; over-give to protect the audience's goodwill.
- **Fits piece type:** content calendar, LinkedIn, short-form, email cadence (judgment).
- **Copywriter uses it by:** planning each batch so asks are a small minority (the mature-platform minimums in the source are about 3.5:1 and 4:1; aim far higher) and keeping value-first pieces ask-free.
- **Editor checks:** Is this piece or batch at or above the give-to-ask threshold? Is the ask kept out of pure-value pieces?
- **Source:** Part II, "Mastering the Give : Ask ratio"

### How to Ask: Integrated vs Intermittent
- **Name:** Two ways to ask: Integrated and Intermittent; plus the two things you advertise (core offer or lead magnet)
- **What it is for:** Weaving promotion into free content. Treat asks like commercials that interrupt your own content.
- **Parts or steps:**
  1. Integrated: advertise inside every piece while keeping the give : ask ratio high.
  2. Intermittent: many pure "give" pieces, then occasionally an "ask" piece (example: 10 give posts, then the 11th promotes).
  3. Then choose what to advertise: your core offer or your lead magnet.
- **Rules and numbers:**
  - Place integrated asks (CTAs) after a valuable moment or at the end of the piece; try one place first, confirm audience growth does not slow, then add the second and so forth.
  - Platform rule: on short platforms intermittent dominates; on long-form platforms integrations are often best.
  - The source marks one boxed example of an integration ("This box is an example of an integration"); the box content was not in the text.
  - "That's it. Don't overcomplicate this." After you ask, get back to providing value.
  - Costs of asking: potential loss of trust, slower growth, and the time to gather the audience. Money-wise it is free.
  - Action Step One: pick integrated or intermittent, then pick core offer or lead magnet. "If you're not sure, do the lead magnet. It's lower risk."
  - Lead magnet pattern: after sharing a way to get more leads, say you have 11 more tips and point to your site to grab a visual of them; the thank-you page after opt-in shows the paid offer with a video. Bonus: lead magnet relevant to the content advertising it.
- **Fits piece type:** lead magnet, long-form, LinkedIn, short-form (judgment).
- **Copywriter uses it by:** choosing one ask mode and one thing to promote per batch, defaulting to lead magnet.
- **Editor checks:** Is the ask placed after a valuable moment or at the end? Is the lead magnet relevant to the piece?
- **Source:** Part II, "How To Make Money From Content: Ask"

### Core offer ask template
- **Name:** Offer example ("go for the jugular") script
- **What it is for:** Direct-response ask built on the offer from the previous chapter.
- **Parts or steps:** Template in order, paraphrased (slot labels are the author's):
  1. State you are looking for a small number (the source says 5) of a specific avatar.
  2. Name the dream outcome they will achieve.
  3. Name the time delay.
  4. Say the best part: they do not have to do the effort and sacrifice.
  5. Add the perceived-likelihood guarantee: if they do not get the dream outcome, you do two things: refund their money, and keep working with them until they get it.
  6. Give the reason: you want everyone to have an amazing experience and are confident you can deliver.
  7. Close with a fairness check ("if that sounds fair") and a call to action: DM me / book a call / comment below / reply to this email.
- **Rules and numbers:** The source says to model the offer on the previous chapter ("go for the jugular" with the core offer, the direct path to the sale). Slot labels map to the value equation (avatar, dream outcome, time delay, effort and sacrifice, perceived likelihood). After you ask, get back to providing value. The brief says do not pitch Five Star or Real Talk in value-first Brett content (project rule): use for company content only (judgment).
- **Fits piece type:** offer post, ad, email (judgment).
- **Copywriter uses it by:** filling each slot with Brett's real client, outcome and guarantee, only where Brett has stated the guarantee.
- **Editor checks:** Are all five slots filled with sourced facts? Is the guarantee real?
- **Source:** Part II, "Offer Example"

### How to Scale It
- **Name:** Depth then width / Width then depth
- **What it is for:** Two opposing, both valid, strategies to scale a warm audience.
- **Parts or steps:**
  - Depth then width (maximize a platform, then move on):
    1. Post content on a relevant platform.
    2. Post content regularly on that platform.
    3. Maximize quality and quantity on that platform.
    4. Add another platform while maintaining quality and quantity on the first.
    5. Repeat steps 1-4 until all relevant platforms are maximized.
  - Width then depth (get on every platform early, then maximize together):
    1. Post content on a relevant platform.
    2. Post content regularly on that platform.
    3. Move to the next relevant platform while maintaining the previous.
    4. Continue until you are on all relevant platforms.
    5. Maximize content creation on all platforms at once.
- **Rules and numbers:**
  - Source frames these as two opposing strategies that both work, each in progressive steps; start scaling after asks begin producing leads and money.
  - Depth step 3 benchmarks: short form may sometimes reach up to ten times per day per platform; long form may reach up to five days per week (soap operas cited).
  - Depth advantages: maximize return on one platform; compounding; fewer resources. Disadvantages: less low-hanging fruit; no feeling of omnipresence; early reliance on a single channel (platforms change or ban without reason).
  - Width advantages: broader audience faster; repurposing (same short video across platforms) captures efficiency. Disadvantages: more labor, attention and time; often lots of bad content everywhere.
  - If you already have a sizable business, scale up faster. Action Step: pick an approach, start posting, then go up the scaling steps over time.
- **Fits piece type:** content strategy and distribution plan (judgment).
- **Copywriter uses it by:** stating which approach a plan follows and the cadence per platform.
- **Editor checks:** Is the first platform at steady cadence before adding another (depth)? Is repurposed content adapted to each platform (width)?
- **Source:** Part II, "How to Scale It"

### 7 Lessons I've Learned From Making Content
- **Name:** 7 Lessons I've Learned From Making Content
- **What it is for:** Rules of practice for creating and using free content.
- **Parts or steps:**
  1. Switch from "How to" to "How I." (and "This is the best way" to "These are my favorite ways"), especially when starting out.
  2. We Need To Be Reminded More Than We Need To Be Taught.
  3. Puddles, Ponds, Lakes, Oceans.
  4. Content Creates Tools For Salespeople.
  5. Free Content Retains Paying Customers.
  6. People don't have shorter attention spans, they have higher standards.
  7. Avoid Pre-Scheduling Posts.
- **Rules and numbers:**
  - 1: Talk about what you have done and what you like, not what others should do; experience cannot be questioned, which makes you bulletproof. Avoids sounding preachy or arrogant. Examples: "How I Built My 7-Figure Agency" vs "How To Build a 7-Figure Agency"; how I make my oatmeal vs how you should; "My favorite way to generate leads" vs "This is the best way to generate leads."
  - 2: Not 100% of the audience listens 100% of the time (author's survey: one in five who saw daily book posts did not know he had a book). Keep repeating yourself; you will tire of your content before the whole audience has seen it.
  - 3: Narrow the focus first. Audiences listen to people with better track records than yours, so a small local business should not start with general business content; own a niche plus place (e.g. plumbing in one town), then expand to local business pond, then brick and mortar chains lake, then general business ocean.
  - 4: Build a master list of "greatest hits"; label each with the problem it solves and the benefit it provides; sales sends them before or after calls, especially content that resolves common prospect concerns.
  - 5: Free content that is good makes customers like you more and stay longer; bad free content makes them like the paid product less. Buyers are more likely to consume free content, and include it in their ROI calculation.
  - 6: Repeats that nothing is too long, only too boring. Streaming shows people binge hours of content they like; biology has not changed, circumstances have (more rewarding options). Make good stuff rather than blame short attention spans.
  - 7: Manually posted content performed better than pre-scheduled in the author's experience; his theory is that the seconds-long feedback loop adds pressure to get it right. He and his team prefer a person to press submit. "Try it."
- **Fits piece type:** all content planning; lesson 3 for positioning; lesson 4 for sales enablement and email (judgment).
- **Copywriter uses it by:** writing in "How I" framing, narrowing topics to Brett's niche and geography, and logging top performers with problem and benefit labels.
- **Editor checks:** Is the piece in "How I" voice rather than prescriptive "you should"? Is the topic scoped to Brett's puddle?
- **Source:** Part II, "7 Lessons I've Learned From Making Content"

### Benchmarks
- **Name:** Benchmarks: size and speed
- **What it is for:** Measuring whether audience-building is working, monthly.
- **Parts or steps:**
  1. Total followers and reach (how big).
  2. Rate of getting followers and reach (how fast), compared month over month.
- **Rules and numbers:**
  - Growth rate formula: New / Started = growth rate (e.g. 500 new / 1000 started = 50%; 5,000 new reach / 10,000 = 50%).
  - Measure both absolute and relative growth.
  - "We can only control inputs." Outputs are only useful if inputs are consistent: pick a posting cadence per platform, pick an ask cadence per platform, start, and "Do. Not. Stop."
  - Examples: 1,000 to 1,500 followers is +500 followers; 10,000 to 15,000 people reached is +5,000 reach. Ten to fifteen listeners in a month is still 50% growth.
  - Pick whichever of absolute or relative growth, the author jokes, makes you feel better; his point is that more ways of measuring give more ways to win (Dr. Kashey, as cited by author). Be consistent, measure a lot, adapt to feedback.
  - Reference (one line): author posted a podcast twice weekly for four years before reaching Top 100; because he did the same thing for years he could trust the feedback. In year five The Game reached Top 10 US business and top 500 worldwide, after 5 years of multiple episodes per week. "Everyone starts at zero."
- **Fits piece type:** reporting and planning (judgment).
- **Copywriter uses it by:** n/a; the editor and CMO use it to report monthly growth.
- **Editor checks:** Are size and rate both reported? Are inputs (cadence) held constant before judging results?
- **Source:** Part II, "Benchmarks - How Well Am I Doing?"

- The content you create is not the compounding asset; the audience is. Part I, opening section.
