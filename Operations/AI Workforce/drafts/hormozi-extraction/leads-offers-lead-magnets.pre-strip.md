### Offer vs. core offer vs. lead magnet (definitions)
- **Name:** Offers, Core Offer, Lead Magnet
- **What it is for:** Deciding what to advertise so leads engage.
- **Parts or steps:**
  1. Offer: what you promise to give in exchange for something of value.
  2. Core offer: your product or service in exchange for money. Advertising it is "the direct path to money."
  3. Lead magnet: "a complete solution to a narrow problem," typically lower-cost or free, used to see who is interested. Once solved, it reveals another problem that your core offer solves.
- **Rules and numbers:** Try advertising the core offer first; it might be all you need. Use a lead magnet first when people want to know more before buying, common with more expensive offers. Your lead magnet should be valuable enough that you could charge for it, and after getting it people should want more of what you offer. Spending time now makes a person more likely to spend money later (the source's idea, reworded). The lead magnet gets them one step closer to buying. Good lead magnets get more engaged leads and customers than a core offer alone, for less money. Analogy: salty pretzels solve hunger, reveal thirst, drink revenue covers the pretzel cost and nets a profit.
- **Fits piece type:** lead magnet, offer, ad (judgment)
- **Copywriter uses it by:** Deciding whether the piece promotes the core offer or a narrow free solution, and writing the promise to match (judgment).
- **Editor checks:** Does the piece name a narrow problem it solves? Does solving it point to the next problem the core offer solves?
- **Source:** Chapter "Engage Your Leads: Offers and Lead Magnets," section "Lead Magnets Get Leads to Engage"

### Seven Steps To Creating an Effective Lead Magnet
- **Name:** Seven Steps To Creating an Effective Lead Magnet
- **What it is for:** Building a lead magnet that gets engaged leads.
- **Parts or steps:**
  1. Step 1: Figure out the problem you want to solve and who to solve it for
  2. Step 2: Figure out how to solve it
  3. Step 3: Figure out how to deliver it
  4. Step 4: Test what to name it
  5. Step 5: Make it easy to consume
  6. Step 6: Make it darn good
  7. Step 7: Make it easy for them to tell you they want more
- **Rules and numbers:** Grand Slam Offers work for free stuff as much or better than for paid stuff. Make the lead magnet "so insanely good people will feel stupid saying no." You may have a few insanely valuable offers, even if some are free. "The business that provides the most value wins." Closing action-step version (same seven, reworded): Step 0: if struggling to get leads, make an amazing lead magnet. Step 1: figure out the problem to solve for the right customer. Step 2: how you want to solve it. Step 3: how to deliver it. Step 4: make the name interesting and clear. Step 5: make it easy to consume. Step 6: make sure it's darn good. Step 7: tell them what to do next, why it's a good idea, do it clearly, and do it often.
- **Fits piece type:** lead magnet, long-form (judgment)
- **Copywriter uses it by:** Using the seven steps as the outline for a lead magnet brief or a how-to post (judgment).
- **Editor checks:** Are all seven steps addressed in order? Is the free value strong enough that saying no feels foolish?
- **Source:** Chapter "Engage Your Leads: Offers and Lead Magnets," sections "Seven Steps To Creating an Effective Lead Magnet" and "Action Steps"

### Problem-Solution cycle (Step 1)
- **Name:** Problem-Solution cycle
- **What it is for:** Picking which problem the lead magnet solves.
- **Parts or steps:**
  1. Pick a problem that is narrow and meaningful.
  2. Solve it.
  3. Solving it reveals a new problem.
  4. If your core offer solves that new problem, you have a winner (you solve it in exchange for money).
- **Rules and numbers:** "Every problem has a solution. Every solution reveals more problems." Smaller problem-solution cycles sit inside larger ones. "Don't overthink it." The step also names who to solve it for. The author's own worked example: this book is the lead magnet; the problem is getting engaged leads; the audience is businesses under $1,000,000 in annual profit; with enough engaged leads they can pass $1,000,000, which qualifies them for his core offer (him investing in their company to help them scale). Real estate illustration in the text: selling a home is broad; narrow problems before it (what the house is worth, increasing value, pictures, cleaning, landscaping, minor fixes, moving, staging) are good lead magnets; solving one free earns trust, then you charge to solve the remaining problems with the core offer. Action Step: pick the narrowly defined problem, then make sure your core offer solves the next problem that comes up.
- **Fits piece type:** lead magnet, LinkedIn post, short-form video (judgment)
- **Copywriter uses it by:** Choosing one narrow pre-sale problem and framing the free piece around it (judgment).
- **Editor checks:** Is the problem narrow rather than broad? Does the core offer solve the problem that appears after this one?
- **Source:** Chapter "Engage Your Leads: Offers and Lead Magnets," Step 1

### Three types of lead magnets (Step 2)
- **Name:** 1) Reveal Problems, 2) Samples and Trials, and 3) One Step Of A Multi-Step Process
- **What it is for:** Choosing how the lead magnet solves the narrow problem. All three solve one problem and reveal others.
- **Parts or steps:**
  1. Reveal Their Problem. Think "diagnosis." Works great when problems get worse the longer you wait. Draw a clear line between where they are and where they should be, and what the gap costs them. Examples given: website speed test (site loads 30% below the speed it should; the line is the money lost), posture analysis (the line is a pain-free life), termite inspection (sell removal if they have termites, prevention if not).
  2. Samples And Trials. Full but brief access to your core offer; limit the number of uses, the time of access, or both. Works great when the core offer is a recurring solution to a recurring problem. Examples: faster server (to keep it they keep paying), free posture adjustment (permanent benefit means buying more), consumable samples (single serving, "fun sized"; Costco sells more food by giving samples).
  3. One Step Of A Multi-Step Process. Give one valuable step free, the rest when they buy. Works great when the core offer solves a more complex problem. Examples: the book itself (it gets you to $1,000,000+ in profit, then new problems the author helps solve), first of three coats of garage sealant (explain it gives only partial coverage, offer the other two as a bundle), free finance courses, guides, calculators, and templates that are valuable enough to do it yourself but reveal the time and effort of doing it all, so you offer financial services.
- **Rules and numbers:** The intro to the step frames the three as: make them aware of a problem they do not know about; solve a recurring problem briefly with a sample or trial; give one step of a bigger solution. Action Step: pick how you want to solve your narrowly defined problem.
- **Fits piece type:** lead magnet, offer, ad (judgment)
- **Copywriter uses it by:** Picking one type and writing the free piece in that shape (diagnosis, sample, or first step) (judgment).
- **Editor checks:** Is the piece clearly one of the three types? If a diagnosis, does it show the cost of waiting?
- **Source:** Chapter "Engage Your Leads: Offers and Lead Magnets," Step 2

### Four delivery methods (Step 3)
- **Name:** Software, Information, Services, Physical Products (four ways to deliver a lead magnet)
- **What it is for:** Choosing the format that delivers the solution. Each works with each of the three types.
- **Parts or steps:**
  1. Software: you give them a tool (spreadsheet, calculator, small software that does a job for them). Author's gym example: a spreadsheet or dashboard that shows a gym owner their business stats, compares them to industry averages, and ranks them.
  2. Information: you teach them something (courses, lessons, expert interviews, keynotes, live events, mistakes and pitfalls, hacks/tips). Author's example: a mini course for gyms on writing an ad.
  3. Services: you do work for free (adjust their back, website audit, first layer of sealant, turn a video into an ebook). Author's example: running a gym owner's ads free for thirty days.
  4. Physical Products: something they can hold (assessment chart, supplement, small bottle of sealant, boxing gloves). Author's example: a book for gym owners, Gym Launch Secrets.
- **Rules and numbers:** Three types times four methods gives up to twelve lead magnets for one narrow problem. Make as many versions as you can and rotate them: keeps advertising fresh and low effort, and shows which work best; results are often surprising. Thought exercise: think of a lead magnet and a version for each delivery method; "You always can." Then pick how to deliver.
- **Fits piece type:** lead magnet, content planning (judgment)
- **Copywriter uses it by:** Generating a version of the same lead magnet in each delivery method to rotate (judgment).
- **Editor checks:** Is the delivery method named and matched to the type? Is there a rotation of versions rather than one?
- **Source:** Chapter "Engage Your Leads: Offers and Lead Magnets," Step 3

### Test what to name it (Step 4)
- **Name:** Test What To Name It (Headline, Image(s), Subheadline)
- **What it is for:** Making leads notice the lead magnet before they can consume it.
- **Parts or steps (test in this order):**
  1. Headline (most important; if you only test one thing, test this)
  2. Image(s)
  3. Subheadline
- **Rules and numbers:** Ogilvy is quoted saying that once the headline is written, 80 cents of the advertising dollar is spent. Five times more people read the headline than any other part of the promotion. Improving the headline, name, and display can 2x, 3x, or 10x engagement. Small changes make big differences: "how to" added two words and beat the version without it; removing "more" beat the version with it. The subtitle stayed on getting strangers to show interest (a raised hand), not on getting them to buy, because "that's sales, not getting leads." How to test: polls if you have any following; otherwise post on every platform and ask for a "1" or "2" and count, or message people and ask. You do not need many votes for a directional idea. Bonus Points: if people ask when they can get it, you have a "mega winner." Action Step: test; if people engage in droves, you have a winner. Name test results shown in the text: Advertising vs Promotion (Advertising won), Advertising vs Leads (Leads won), Marketing vs Leads (Leads won); image: Real beat Cartoon. Subheadline rounds also shown (layout garbled in the text): the winner each round was "How to get strangers to want to buy your stuff," beating a version without "how to," a version with "more" added, a "more people" version, and "How to get as many leads as you darn well please." The author's two notes on the subheadline tests: two small words ("how to") and one removed word ("more") made a big difference; and he avoided "buy" in the subtitle because that is sales, not leads.
- **Fits piece type:** headline, ad, short-form video hook, LinkedIn post opener (judgment)
- **Copywriter uses it by:** Writing several headline options, then image and subheadline options, for polling (judgment).
- **Editor checks:** Does the piece offer at least two headline options to test? Does the subheadline promise engagement, not a purchase, when the goal is leads?
- **Source:** Chapter "Engage Your Leads: Offers and Lead Magnets," Step 4

### Make it easy to consume (Step 5)
- **Name:** Make it easy for them to consume (by delivery method)
- **What it is for:** Raising take rates and consumption.
- **Parts or steps:**
  1. Software: accessible on phones, on a computer, and in multiple formats.
  2. Information: offer it in as many formats as you can (images, video, text, audio); people prefer watching, reading, or listening.
  3. Services: be available at more times in more ways (more times of day, more days, video call, phone call, in person).
  4. Physical products: simple to order, fast to arrive, easy to open, simple directions. Source example: Apple products need no directions and the packaging is good enough that most people keep the boxes.
- **Rules and numbers:** "People prefer to do things that take less effort." Easier consumption can give 2x, 3x, even 4x+ increases in take rates and consumption. For services, the easier you are to reach, the more likely people become engaged leads. Action Step: package the lead magnet in every way you can; more formats means more engaged leads and more people getting value. Fun fact in the source: his book $100M Offers splits roughly evenly across ebook, physical, audiobook, and video (video free on Acquisition.com); he says multiple formats is the easiest way he knows to get 2-3-4x the leads for the same work, and one format alone would miss the 3-4x who would not otherwise read it.
- **Fits piece type:** lead magnet, long-form (judgment)
- **Copywriter uses it by:** Planning the same lead magnet as video, text, and audio versions (judgment).
- **Editor checks:** Is it offered in more than one format? Is the access path low-effort?
- **Source:** Chapter "Engage Your Leads: Offers and Lead Magnets," Step 5

### Give Away The Secrets, Sell The Implementation (Step 6)
- **Name:** Give Away The Secrets, Sell The Implementation
- **What it is for:** Setting the quality bar for the lead magnet.
- **Parts or steps:** (stated as a rule, not a numbered list)
  1. Make the lead magnet so valuable that people feel they owe you payment.
  2. Deliver more value than your core offer costs, before they buy.
- **Rules and numbers:** The marketplace judges everything you offer, free or not. "You can never provide too much value. But, you can provide too little." Giving away sucky fluff makes people think you have nothing and they buy elsewhere and tell others not to buy from you. People buy based on how much value they think they will get after they buy; the easiest way to create that belief is to provide value before they buy. "99% of people aren't gonna buy," but they create or destroy your reputation based on the free stuff. Make lead magnets as good as paid stuff. Source illustration: a company that scaled from $1M to $10M on his free content is very likely to partner with him, because he paid for his share before they started. Closing line of the step: provide value, stack the deck, reap the rewards.
- **Fits piece type:** lead magnet, long-form, LinkedIn post (judgment)
- **Copywriter uses it by:** Putting real method and detail in the free piece and leaving the doing-it-for-you to the paid offer (judgment).
- **Editor checks:** Would the free piece stand beside the paid work without embarrassment? Does it teach the how, not just tease it?
- **Source:** Chapter "Engage Your Leads: Offers and Lead Magnets," Step 6

### Call To Action (CTA) (Step 7)
- **Name:** Call To Action (CTA): what to do + reasons to do it right now
- **What it is for:** Letting leads who consumed the lead magnet say they want more.
- **Parts or steps:**
  1. What to do: call the number, click the button, give information, book the call, etc. Use clear, simple, direct language. Quoted contrast: not "don't delay" but "call now."
  2. Reasons to do it right now. Good reasons work better than bad reasons; any reason, even bad, tends to beat no reason.
- **Rules and numbers:** A good CTA pairs 1) what to do with 2) reasons to act right now. Include as many effective reasons as possible. Action Step: give a clear, simple, action-oriented CTA, then a "reason why" using scarcity, urgency, and any other reasons, and do it often. "Don't be clever, be clear."
- **Fits piece type:** all pieces with an ask: ad, email, post close, offer (judgment)
- **Copywriter uses it by:** Ending the piece with one plain action plus one reason to act now (judgment).
- **Editor checks:** Is the action one clear verb phrase? Is there a stated reason to act now?
- **Source:** Chapter "Engage Your Leads: Offers and Lead Magnets," Step 7

### Reasons to act now: Scarcity, Urgency, Fraternity Party Planner (Step 7)
- **Name:** a) Scarcity, b) Urgency, c) Fraternity Party Planner (Make Up A Reason)
- **What it is for:** Supplying the "reason to do it right now" in a CTA.
- **Parts or steps:**
  1. Scarcity: a limited amount, especially small supply vs demand. People want scarce things more and act sooner. The catch: the fewer you have, the fewer engaged leads you can get before running out, so the best strategy is "reality": if you sold 1000x the customers tomorrow could you handle it? If not, you have a real limit (customer service, onboarding, inventory, time slots per week). Advertise it; this gives "ethical scarcity." Examples: class times fill up fast; "I can only handle five people per week"; one batch of shirts, never reprinted.
  2. Urgency: people act faster when they have a short time, and the shorter the time, the faster they act. Shorten the time to act on the CTA. Also use discounts or bonuses that go away after X minutes or hours, after which the offer is never available again. Examples: July 4th promotion ends Monday at midnight; Black Friday promotion ends at midnight with four hours left; free hat through Friday to anyone buying more than three books.
  3. Fraternity Party Planner (his favorite): make up a reason. Fraternities need no reason to party but invent doozies (a wisdom-teeth kegger, Margherita Monday, Toga Tuesdays, Thirsty Thursday). The reason does not have to make sense. A Harvard experiment (letting someone cut in line) showed more people complied when given a reason; the number rose if the reason made sense (like scarcity and urgency), but any reason beat none. Think "the stuff you say" after the word because. Examples: because moms know best; because your country needs you; because it is my birthday and I want you to celebrate.
- **Rules and numbers:** Always try to include a reason. Scarcity should be real, from natural limits in the business; the source says to advertise the limit, not keep it a secret ("if you have limitations you may as well use them to make money," paraphrased).
- **Fits piece type:** ad, email, short-form video close, offer (judgment)
- **Copywriter uses it by:** Picking the true limit or deadline that exists in Brett's business and stating it in the CTA (judgment).
- **Editor checks:** Is the scarcity or deadline real and stated? If neither applies, is at least one reason given?
- **Source:** Chapter "Engage Your Leads: Offers and Lead Magnets," Step 7

### Lead magnet cost-to-acquire math
- **Name:** Lead magnet versus core offer advertising math (worked formula)
- **What it is for:** Showing that a lead magnet lowers cost to get a customer.
- **Parts or steps (the source's worked numbers):**
  1. Core offer profit $10,000. Advertising cost to get someone on a call: $1,000. Close 1 of 3, so customer cost = $3,000.
  2. Advertise a free lead magnet that costs $25 to deliver. Advertising cost to get someone on a call: $75. All in: $100 per call.
  3. If 1 in 10 who get the lead magnet buy the core offer: $100 x 10 = $1,000 to acquire a customer.
  4. Result: customer cost falls 3x ($3,000 to $1,000); with $10,000 profit that is a 10:1 return; same ad budget, triple the business.
- **Rules and numbers:** Even if the lead magnet costs money to deliver, it should lower the cost to get a new customer. More engaged leads means more chances to get customers; the extra customers cover the costs. "The goal is to print money, not just make our 'fair share.'" A $25 delivery budget lets you give far more value than $0. A lead magnet can get ten times more engaged leads for the same cost; "this happens all the time when you nail the lead magnet." The source adds that this is where experienced owners beat newbies: the lead magnet is more valuable than competitors', so more strangers become engaged leads and more buy because value came first ("Win. Win. Win."). These figures are the author's illustration, not benchmarks for Brett.
- **Fits piece type:** long-form, LinkedIn post, internal planning (judgment)
- **Copywriter uses it by:** Explaining the logic with the author's sequence, but any figures must be Brett's own sourced numbers (judgment).
- **Editor checks:** Is every dollar figure sourced from Brett or labeled as the book's illustration?
- **Source:** Chapter "Engage Your Leads: Offers and Lead Magnets," closing section before "Action Steps"

### A good lead magnet does four things
- **Name:** Four things a good lead magnet does (Section II Conclusion)
- **What it is for:** Checklist for judging a lead magnet.
- **Parts or steps:**
  1. Engages ideal customers when they see it.
  2. Gets more people to engage than your core offer alone.
  3. Is valuable enough that they consume it.
  4. Makes the right people more likely to buy.
- **Rules and numbers:** Result stated: more people show interest, more money from them, more value delivered, all at once. Section II framing: leads alone are not enough, you need engaged leads (first chapter); a valuable lead magnet or offer gets leads to engage (this chapter).
- **Fits piece type:** lead magnet (editor checklist) (judgment)
- **Copywriter uses it by:** Self-checking a draft lead magnet against the four before handing it over (judgment).
- **Editor checks:** Does it meet all four? Is the audience the right customer, not just any lead?
- **Source:** Section II Conclusion

## Standalone rules
- "Get leads to engage" by giving people something they want; "it's easier than you think." Source: section "The case study" (lesson stated at the end of that section).
- Advertising your core offer is the first thing to try, before a lead magnet. Source: "Lead Magnets Get Leads to Engage."
- Lead magnets suit more expensive offers, where people want to know more before buying. Source: "Lead Magnets Get Leads to Engage."
- A lead who takes a cheap or free offer today is more likely to buy a related pricier one later. Source: "Lead Magnets Get Leads to Engage."
- Presentation matters more than anything: a lead has to notice the magnet before consuming it, and an ignored magnet never gets judged on quality. Source: Step 4.
- Test the packaging of the lead magnet because it is "one of the highest leverage things" for your time. Source: Step 4.
- Subheadline goal for a lead book: get a raised hand (interest), not a sale; one problem at a time. Source: Step 4.
- Scarcity and urgency reasons are both usable together with any other reason; "do it often." Source: Step 7.
- Giving time first makes a person likelier to give money later. Source: "Lead Magnets Get Leads to Engage."
