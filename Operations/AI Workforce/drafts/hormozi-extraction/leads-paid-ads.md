### Paid ads: the core logic (needles in the haystack)
- **Name:** How Paid Ads Work / finding needles in the haystack
- **What it is for:** Explains what paid ads are and the order of narrowing from the whole world to the right buyers.
- **Parts or steps:**
  1. First: choose a platform that holds my ideal audience.
  2. Second: use the platform's own targeting methods to find them.
  3. Third: write the ad so it repels everyone else.
  4. Finally: tell whoever remains to take the next step.
- **Rules and numbers:**
  - Paid ads are one-to-many advertising to cold audiences (people who do not know you): you pay another person or business to put your offer in front of their audience, "renting eyeballs or earballs"; you trade money for reach. No need to spend time building an audience, so it is the fastest way to get the most people to see your stuff. Ads are riskier, but done right can get more leads than any other method.
  - Warm and cold outreach need more work to reach more people; free content depends on the platform or audience sharing it. With paid ads reach is guaranteed, getting your money back is not, so it is a game of efficiency rather than reach.
  - In principle, paying enough would get every person in the world to see the ad, and someone would buy, even by accident. So the question is how well ads can be made to work, a push and pull between how much you spend and how much they buy.
  - Like cold outreach, paid ads go to colder, lower-trust audiences, so even with good offers a smaller percentage respond; ads overcome this by putting the offer in front of more people.
  - If an ad is not profitable, most of the time the right people never saw it; the right people have to see it to keep ads efficient.
  - Start profitable in a small audience (puddle), then expand to pond, lake, ocean. A bigger audience has more wrong people but also more right ones, so efficiency (ratio of spend to sales) drops but total profit rises. Example: $1,000 to make $10,000 ($9,000 profit) versus $100,000 to make $300,000 ($200,000 profit). Risk is higher because you spend more, and so is the reward.
  - Make the audience as big as possible while still turning a profit.
  - Paid ads add four problems to solve: 1) Knowing where to advertise, 2) Getting the right audience to see it, 3) Making the best ad for them to see, 4) Getting permission to contact them.
- **Fits piece type (judgment):** Long-form explainer, LinkedIn post on why ads are an efficiency game.
- **Copywriter uses it by:** Using the four-problem list and the puddle-to-ocean idea as the spine of an explainer.
- **Editor checks:** Does the piece say ads are judged on efficiency, not reach? Are all four problems named in order?
- **Source:** $100M Leads, Part I, "How Paid Ads Work".

### Step 1: Find a platform where these four things are true
- **Name:** Step 1: "But where do I advertise?" (platform selection)
- **What it is for:** Choosing where to run ads.
- **Parts or steps:**
  1. I have used it as a consumer and gotten value from it, so I understand how it works.
  2. I can target people on the platform who are interested in my stuff.
  3. I know how to format ads specific to the platform (covered in Step 3).
  4. I have the minimum amount of money needed to place an ad.
- **Rules and numbers:** Platforms distribute content to an audience, and wherever there is an audience you can usually advertise; to become a great entrepreneur you have to learn about them. Platforms change all the time, but these principles stay the same. Action step: start with one platform that meets the four requirements, and start watching, listening to, or reading ads on it.
- **Fits piece type (judgment):** Checklist post, LinkedIn post.
- **Copywriter uses it by:** Turning the four requirements into a "before you spend a dollar" checklist.
- **Editor checks:** Are all four criteria present and in order? Does it avoid recommending a platform the author did not name?
- **Source:** $100M Leads, Part I, Step 1.

### Step #2: Target them (two ways to target)
- **Name:** Step #2: "But how do I get the right people to see it?" → Target them
- **What it is for:** Narrowing the audience inside the chosen platform.
- **Parts or steps:**
  1. Target a lookalike audience (platform shows your ad to an audience similar to, and much bigger than, a list you upload).
  2. Target with factors of your choosing (age, income, gender, interests, time, location, etc.).
- **Rules and numbers:**
  - One goal when targeting: get the highest number of people you think will buy to see your ad. The platform choice is the first round of targeting; this is the second.
  - The two methods can be used separately or combined.
  - Lookalike list order: start with current and previous customers; if too small for the platform minimum, add the warm reach out list; if still too small, add cold reach out leads. Bigger and higher quality list means a more responsive lookalike audience. Forcing the list to size can make the lookalike too broad, which is OK because you can add filters.
  - The right message to the wrong audience falls on deaf ears no matter how good the ad.
  - Example: if no one over forty-five or below twenty-five has ever bought, exclude anyone outside that range. Basic filters on top of the platform-generated lookalike are a simple way to get more of the right people to see the ad.
  - More filters means a more specific list, more efficient ads, but you burn through it faster. Early wins from small specific audiences fund broader audiences later: "This is how you scale."
  - Action step: put all lead lists in one place, separated into past and previous customers, warm outreach, cold outreach (later add people who engaged with paid ads but did not buy); use them in order of quality; add filters on top. If you cannot make a lookalike, start by targeting interests.
- **Fits piece type (judgment):** Long-form, how-to post.
- **Copywriter uses it by:** Explaining the lookalike list build order and the specific-then-broad scaling logic.
- **Editor checks:** Is the list order (customers, warm, cold) intact? Is the specificity-versus-burn-rate tradeoff stated?
- **Source:** $100M Leads, Part I, Step #2.

### Step #3: Call Out + Value + Call to Action (CTA)
- **Name:** Call Out + Value + Call to Action (CTA) (the three chunks of an ad)
- **What it is for:** The structure of every ad.
- **Parts or steps:**
  1. Call Outs: I need to get them to notice my ad.
  2. Value: I need to get them interested in what I have to offer.
  3. Calls to Action: I need to tell them what to do next.
- **Rules and numbers:**
  - Restated in the Part I conclusion as the three core elements of every ad: 1) Callouts (for them to notice it), 2) Value Elements (to give them reason to do something), 3) Calls to action (to give them a way to do it). Contact capture (Step #4) then makes them an engaged lead.
  - Long versus short ads: the only difference is how many angles of the copywriting framework you have time to cover. Add or remove angles by platform, but keep the callouts (first few seconds) and CTAs the same.
- **Fits piece type (judgment):** Ad, short-form video script.
- **Copywriter uses it by:** Drafting every ad in three labeled blocks before polishing.
- **Editor checks:** Does the draft have a callout, a value block, and a CTA? Do the callout and CTA stay constant across length versions?
- **Source:** $100M Leads, Part I, Step #3 and "Run Paid Ads Part I Conclusion".

### Call Outs (verbal and nonverbal)
- **Name:** Call Out (verbal callouts and nonverbal callouts)
- **What it is for:** Getting the right people to notice the ad (the "cocktail party effect").
- **Parts or steps (verbal callouts, using words to get attention):**
  1. Labels: a word or set of words putting people into a group (features, traits, titles, places, descriptors). Example: "Clark County Moms". Ideal customers need to identify with the label.
     - Local rule: people automatically identify with their local area; the more local the better. "LOCAL AREA + TYPE OF PERSON" is one of the author's all-time favorites.
  2. Yes-Questions: questions where answering "yes, that's me" qualifies them for the offer. Example: "Do you have a home worth over $400,000?"
  3. If-Then Statements: if they meet your conditions, you help them make a decision. Example: "If you run over $100,000 per month in ads, we can save you 20% or more".
  4. Ridiculous Results: bizarre, rare, or out-of-the-ordinary stuff someone would want. Example: "Massage studio books out two years in advance. Clients furious."
- **Parts or steps (nonverbal callouts, using the setting and spokesperson):**
  1. Contrast: anything that "sticks out" in the first few seconds (colors, sounds, movements). Bright shirt over dull; attractive over plain; moving over still.
  2. Likeness: visually showing labels (features, traits, titles, places, descriptors) people identify with. People prefer to work with those whose look, talk, and behavior feel familiar, and you may not be familiar to them. Broad customer base: use more ethnicities, ages, genders, personalities. Narrow customer base: use people who look like them. "Quack like a duck": to attract plumbers, dress, talk, and be in the environment of a plumber; same message does far better if you look the part or find people who do. Mascots work because they don't age, ask for more money, or take days off. Advanced: if the face is not you, the business depends less on you and is more sellable; attractive people convert better and it is cheap to hire one to speak to a camera.
  3. The Scene: visually showing the Yes-Questions and If-Then statements. Example: person tossing and turning in bed (sleep trouble).
- **Rules and numbers:**
  - Callouts do not have to be words; they can be noises or visuals in the environment. If they never notice the ad, nothing else matters.
  - Each second of the ad exists to sell the second after it. The headline is the first sale.
  - Author's advertising became "20x more effective" when most effort went to the first five seconds.
  - Goal: a callout specific enough to get the right people and broad enough to get as many of them as possible. Callouts range from hyperspecific (one person) to not specific (everyone).
  - Use verbal and nonverbal callouts together when the platform allows.
  - Ogilvy quote the author uses: "After you've written your headline, you've spent eighty cents of your advertising dollar." Focus effort front to back.
  - The list is explicitly not exhaustive.
  - Action step: stop skipping ads; study the callouts.
- **Fits piece type (judgment):** Ad, short-form video hook, LinkedIn hook line, email subject line.
- **Copywriter uses it by:** Writing one hook per verbal type (label, yes-question, if-then, ridiculous result) and pairing with a visual cue.
- **Editor checks:** Does the first line name or qualify the intended audience? Does the opening earn the next second?
- **Source:** $100M Leads, Part I, Step #3, "1) Call Out".

### What-Who-When Framework (Value, "Get Them Interested")
- **Name:** What-Who-When Framework
- **What it is for:** Answering "why should I be interested?" by making benefits look as big and costs as small as possible, then building many angles.
- **Parts or steps:**
  1. The What: eight key elements (value equation): how the offer delivers each of four value elements and avoids their four opposites.
  2. The Who: whose perspective sees the change in status (the customer gaining status; the people giving it).
  3. The When: the time period of the experience (past, present, future).
- **Rules and numbers:**
  - People value an offer or lead magnet when benefits look big and costs tiny; they exchange money or contact info for it. If cost outweighs benefits they do not value it. The best ads make benefits look as big and costs as small as possible, which gets the most engaged leads. A good ad (paid or not) answers "why should I be interested in your thing?" The framework hinges on knowing the value equation forwards and backwards; "hidden costs" are referenced from an earlier chapter.
  - Ads make the prospect think questions to themselves; a good ad answers them at the moment they think them.
  - The author says he includes "a few more ad tips and tricks" in lessons at the end of the chapter; the text does not say which lessons these are.
  - Each new Who perspective can be applied to each value driver, and each can be run through its own timeline, giving many stories and angles. The more angles covered, the more interested they become.
  - Go both toward good stuff and away from bad stuff (carrots versus sticks).
  - Write in the words the prospect would use; answer the questions they think at the moment they think them.
  - Action steps: What = know the eight key things about your product, how it fulfills each element and avoids its opposite; Who = show how they change the prospect's status, then how the people they know give status or take it away; When = show consequences of buying and not buying through past, present, future, especially change in status with people they know.
- **Fits piece type (judgment):** Ad, long-form, offer page, video script.
- **Copywriter uses it by:** Building a grid of 8 elements x who x when and picking the strongest angles per piece.
- **Editor checks:** Does the piece cover value and its opposite, not only the benefit? Is at least one other person's perspective or a timeline shift used?
- **Source:** $100M Leads, Part I, Step #3, "2) Get Them Interested".

### The What: Eight Key Elements
- **Name:** The What: Eight Key Elements (four value elements and their four opposites)
- **What it is for:** The content inventory for the value chunk of an ad.
- **Parts or steps (author's order):**
  1. Dream Outcome: show and tell the maximum benefit, aligned with the ideal prospect's dream outcome (the results they experience after buying).
  2. Opposite - Nightmare: the worst hassles and pain of going without your solution; the bad stuff they will experience if they do not buy.
  3. Perceived Likelihood of Achievement: because of past failures people assume even buying carries a risk of not getting what they want. Lower perceived risk by minimizing or explaining away past failures, emphasizing success of people like them, authority assurances, guarantees, a better chance than what they do now.
  4. Opposite - Risk: how risky it is not to act; what life looks like if they carry on as always; they repeat past failures and problems get bigger and worse.
  5. Time Delay: how slow their current trajectory is, or that they will never get there at their current rate.
  6. Opposite - Speed: getting things takes time; show how much faster they get the thing they want.
  7. Effort and Sacrifice: the work and skill needed without your solution, continued giving up of loved things and suffering from hated things, and that they may work hard now and have gotten nowhere (they waste more time and money than if they bought the solution).
  8. Opposite - Ease: people assume change means doing hated things and giving up loved things; ease comes from lack of needed work or skill. Disprove the assumption; show they avoid hated work, do more of what they love, without hard work or skill, and still get the dream outcome.
- **Rules and numbers:** The author's own labeling is as above (each element followed by its "Opposite"); he calls the set "the 8 key elements" and says they are the four value elements plus avoiding their four opposites. "Hidden costs" is referenced from an earlier chapter and is not defined in this file.
- **Fits piece type (judgment):** Ad, offer, lead magnet, long-form.
- **Copywriter uses it by:** Writing one line per element about the specific offer.
- **Editor checks:** Are all eight elements considered? Does the piece include both sides of at least two pairs?
- **Source:** $100M Leads, Part I, Step #3, "The What: Eight Key Elements".

### The Who (status groups)
- **Name:** The Who
- **What it is for:** Showing how the product changes how other people treat the customer.
- **Parts or steps:**
  1. Group gaining status: your customers.
  2. Group giving status: Spouse, Kids, Parents, Extended Family, Colleagues, Bosses, Friends, Rivals, Competitors, etc.
- **Rules and numbers:** People are driven mainly by status, and a person's status comes from how others treat them. If the product changes how others treat the customer (it does in some way), show how. Talk about each value element from someone else's perspective to show every way the customer's status improves. Different perspectives give new angles and bonus benefits. Each new Who perspective can be applied to each value driver, which yields many stories, examples, and angles (more carrot, less stick).
  - Example: for "risk free", the spouse will not nag about the purchase.
- **Fits piece type (judgment):** Ad, long-form, email.
- **Copywriter uses it by:** Rewriting one benefit through the spouse, kids, boss, and rival.
- **Editor checks:** Does at least one benefit appear through another person's eyes?
- **Source:** $100M Leads, Part I, Step #3, "Who".

### The When (past, present, future)
- **Name:** The When
- **What it is for:** Making the consequences of acting or not acting visible across time.
- **Parts or steps:**
  1. Past
  2. Present
  3. Future
- **Rules and numbers:** People often think only of the here and now; also explain what decisions led to in the past and could lead to in the future, so they see the consequences of their decision or indecision right now. Run the timeline through the prospect's own perspective, then through other people's perspectives.
  - Example (WHO/WHAT/WHEN): the spouse (Who) notices how fast (What) they fit into the suit the wife loves (When: future).
  - Show the bad stuff they want to avoid first, then contrast with the good stuff (present and future) if they buy. Combine toward-good and away-from-bad with the timeline to create "powerful motivators in our copy". Putting What, Who, and When together answers WHY they should be interested.
- **Fits piece type (judgment):** Ad, story-style post, email.
- **Copywriter uses it by:** Writing a before, now, after beat for the prospect and one other person.
- **Editor checks:** Does the piece show consequences over time, not just the present?
- **Source:** $100M Leads, Part I, Step #3, "When".

### CTA: Tell Them What To Do Next
- **Name:** 3) CTA - Tell Them What To Do Next
- **What it is for:** Converting short-lived motivation into action.
- **Parts or steps:** Spell out the action. Examples in the author's order: Click this button. Call this number. Reply with "YES." Go to this website. Scan this QR Code.
- **Rules and numbers:**
  - If the ad got them interested, they have huge motivation for a tiny time. The audience only knows what to do if you tell them.
  - Spell it out: many ads still do not tell the audience what to do.
  - Make CTAs quick and easy: easy phone numbers, obvious buttons, simple websites. Short memorable web address.
  - Strengthen CTAs with urgency, scarcity, and bonuses from "Step 7" of the "engage your leads" chapter (that chapter is not in this file).
- **Fits piece type (judgment):** Ad, any post ending, email.
- **Copywriter uses it by:** Ending every piece with one literal instruction and one short link or word.
- **Editor checks:** Is there exactly one clear next action? Is the link or word short enough to remember?
- **Source:** $100M Leads, Part I, Step #3, "3) CTA".

### Step #4: Get Permission To Contact Them
- **Name:** Step #4: "How do I get their info?" → Get Permission To Contact Them
- **What it is for:** Capturing contact information to create an engaged lead.
- **Parts or steps:** After they take the action, get their contact information. Use a simple landing page (the author's "three favorite templates" are images not present in the text; pick one and start testing). Focus on the words and the image.
- **Rules and numbers:**
  - Simpler landing page is easier to test.
  - Make the landing page match the ad: same look and language; deliver what the ad promised. Avoid a "Frankenstein experience"; aim for a continuous experience "from click to close".
  - Get more people through more steps. Consistency principle (credited to Cialdini, Influence): people like to see themselves as consistent, so remind them of the action just taken (CTA) and show the next action aligns with it, which gets more people to give contact info. Patterns: "Now that you just did A, you need to do B" (to get the most of A); or doing A makes them a "doing A" kind of person, and that kind of person does B.
  - Not selling; asking if they are interested. If they give contact info they become engaged leads.
  - Action step: build the first landing page. Drag-and-drop tools build sites in minutes and freelancers build them cheaply.
  - Part I conclusion sequence: show the ad to the right people (platform, targeting), get them to notice it, give them a reason to act now (value equation across past, present, future, from their view and the views of people they know), then a way to give permission to contact, which makes them an engaged lead.
- **Fits piece type (judgment):** Landing page, lead magnet, ad.
- **Copywriter uses it by:** Copying the ad's headline words onto the landing page and adding an "A so B" line.
- **Editor checks:** Does the landing page repeat the ad's promise? Does it ask only for contact info, not a sale?
- **Source:** $100M Leads, Part I, Step #4.

### The Three Phases of Scaling Paid Ads
- **Name:** The Three Phases of Scaling Paid Ads
- **What it is for:** How much to spend and when.
- **Parts or steps:**
  1. Phase One: Track Money. Before spending a dollar, set up accurate tracking of returns (consultant, tutorials). Then do more of what makes money and less of what does not.
  2. Phase Two: Lose Money (author prefers "investing in a money printing machine"). You pay first, so the bank account goes down before it comes up.
  3. Phase Three: Print Money. If you make back more than you spend, spend as much as you can.
- **Rules and numbers:**
  - Part II opens with four questions: How much do I spend? (Three Phases of Scaling Ads); How do I know how well I'm doing? (Cost & Benchmarks); If my ads aren't profitable, how do I fix it? (Client Financed Acquisition); What do I wish I had known before my first paid ad? (Lessons).
  - Phase One: not tracking means getting "cleaned out"; tracking lets you do more of what makes money and less of what does not.
  - Phase Two: losses are many but small because he knows when to shut down; wins are few but large because he knows when to hit the gas.
  - Phase Two testing budget: two times the cash collected from a customer in thirty days (not LTGP). Example: $100 profit in 30 days means up to $200 spend before shutting off, as long as you are getting leads. If an ad gets no leads at all, shut it off before spending 1x thirty-day cash ($100 in the example).
  - Lose often but small, win rarely but big: example, ten ads at $100 each ($1,000 total), nine lose all $100, one returns $500 on its $100, so you are still down $500; many stop there, but the winner is the signal. Spend $10,000 on the winning ad and make $50,000 back (100x down). You may lose nine or ninety-nine times in a row before winning big. Double, triple, quadruple, 10x down on winners.
  - It costs money to build an advertising machine and that is normal. Measure returns over a long time horizon, not next week.
  - Phase Three: if you make back more than you spend, spend as much as you can, but realistically another business constraint limits customers. Ask "How many customers do I want?" or "How many customers can I handle?" rather than "How much should I spend on an ad?"; once ads break even or better, reverse the budget from the sales goal.
  - Phase Three budget method: customers x cost to acquire, plus a twenty percent pad because ads get less efficient as they scale. Example: 100 customers x $100 = $10,000, padded to $12,000 over 30 days = $400 per day. Commit to it. "If the number terrifies you, then you're doing it right." Trust the data; this is how you scale and why most people never do.
- **Fits piece type (judgment):** Long-form, LinkedIn post, offer explainer.
- **Copywriter uses it by:** Using the three phase labels and the 2x thirty-day rule as the backbone.
- **Editor checks:** Are the phases named exactly and in order? Is the 2x thirty-day cash rule kept with "not LTGP"?
- **Source:** $100M Leads, Part II, "The Three Phases of Scaling Paid Ads".

### LTGP to CAC
- **Name:** LTGP to CAC (lifetime gross profit to customer acquisition cost)
- **What it is for:** Measuring paid ad efficiency.
- **Parts or steps:**
  1. LTGP = a customer's total lifetime spend minus the total cost to deliver it. (Example: $15 item, $5 to deliver, so $10 gross profit; ten purchases: $150 - $50 = $100 LTGP.)
  2. CAC = cost to acquire a customer.
  3. Express as the LTGP to CAC ratio. LTGP greater than CAC = profitable advertising; lower = losing money.
- **Rules and numbers:**
  - Use LTGP, not "Lifetime Value" or LTV. Gross profit is the actual money used to acquire customers, pay rent, payroll, and everything else.
  - Every struggling-to-scale business the author invested in had LTGP to CAC under 3 to 1; above 3 to 1 they take off. He states this as an observed pattern, "not a rule".
  - Efficient paid ads make more money than they cost. When the author says "I'm getting 3 to 1 on this" he means LTGP to CAC. If LTGP is greater than CAC the advertising is profitable; lower means losing money.
  - Two levers: make CAC lower (cheaper customers, via more efficient ads following the steps above) and make LTGP higher (more made per customer, via a better business model). He prefers both.
  - Many entrepreneurs think they have crappy ads (high CAC) when they have a crappy business model (low LTGP). CAC between competitors in the same industry is much closer than expected; winners differ in how much they make per customer.
  - Diagnosis rule: research industry average CAC. Per the source: CAC below 3x the industry average is labeled "good" and means focus on the business model (LTGP); CAC above 3x the average is labeled "bad" and means focus on advertising (CAC).
  - Lowering CAC by $100 eventually takes more work than making $100 more per customer; costs can only approach zero, revenue can grow without limit.
- **Fits piece type (judgment):** Long-form, LinkedIn post, offer or pricing piece.
- **Copywriter uses it by:** Teaching the ratio and the 3x diagnosis as a decision rule.
- **Editor checks:** Is LTGP (not LTV) used and defined? Is the 3 to 1 labeled as an observed pattern, not a law?
- **Source:** $100M Leads, Part II, "How well am I doing? Cost & Returns - Efficiency Benchmarks".

### Client Financed Acquisition
- **Name:** Client Financed Acquisition
- **What it is for:** Fixing cash flow when LTGP exceeds CAC but profit arrives slowly.
- **Parts or steps:**
  1. Get the customer's first-30-day spend to exceed what it cost to acquire and fulfill them.
  2. Use that cash to pay off the 30-day "card" balance and repeat.
  3. Method the author uses to get there: immediately sell them more stuff (upsell).
- **Rules and numbers:**
  - The problem: LTGP is often bigger than CAC, but not after the first purchase; first-purchase profit is often less than CAC, so profit arrives over many months. That cash flow problem cripples scaling. If the first 30 days cover acquisition and fulfillment, you have the funds to scale now and forever.
  - Paying off the card leaves zero debt and a new customer to keep profiting from; money stops being the bottleneck ("key to limitless scale").
  - Thirty days chosen because any business can get interest-free money for thirty days in the form of a credit card.
  - Worked numbers: $15 per month membership, $5 cost = $10 gross profit per month; ten-month stay = $100 LTGP; CAC $30 = 3.3:1; but only $10 comes back in month one. Add a $100 upsell at 100% margin taken by one in five: $20 average per customer, so $10 + $20 = $30 in 30 days = CAC, break even ("free customers").
  - Without the upsell, you spend $30 and get $10 back, and the rest trickles in monthly. It creates a cash flow problem. What can be sold or upsold is unlimited.
  - Bottom line: find a way for customers to repay you within thirty days, then recycle the cash into more customers.
- **Fits piece type (judgment):** Offer, long-form, pricing piece.
- **Copywriter uses it by:** Framing the first 30 days as the payback window and the upsell as the lever.
- **Editor checks:** Is the 30-day window stated? Are figures labeled as the author's example?
- **Source:** $100M Leads, Part II, "My ads aren't profitable, how do I fix it? Client Financed Acquisition".

### Personal Lessons from Paid Ads
- **Name:** Personal Lessons from Paid Ads (three lessons)
- **What it is for:** Rules the author names from experience.
- **Parts or steps:**
  1. Don't Confuse Sales Problems With Advertising Problems.
  2. Your Best Free Content Can Make The Best Paid Ads (includes a. User Generated Content (UGC)).
  3. If You Say You Suck At Something, You Will Probably Suck At It.
- **Rules and numbers:**
  - 1: Acquisition cost does not only come from advertising (it mostly does). Test: leads who have the problem and the money but do not buy mean the ads are fine and the sales process is the problem.

UGC (customer testimonials or reviews) that performs as free content often makes a strong ad; a system encouraging public customer posts is his favorite way to get a steady stream of potential ads, at no extra work.
  - 3: Never say "I'm not techy" or "I hate tech stuff"; it keeps you poorer than you should be.
- **Fits piece type (judgment):** LinkedIn post, short-form video.
- **Copywriter uses it by:** One post per lesson.
- **Editor checks:** Is each rule stated plainly with its condition? Is no author anecdote reused as Brett's own?
- **Source:** $100M Leads, Part II, "Personal Lessons from Paid Ads".

### Your Turn: first $100 ad and Sample Paid Ads Checklist
- **Name:** Your Turn (place one ad for $100) and Sample Paid Ads Checklist
- **What it is for:** Getting started.
- **Parts or steps:**
  1. Search "HOW TO PLACE A [PLATFORM] AD."
