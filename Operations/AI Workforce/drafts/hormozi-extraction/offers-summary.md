## SECTION I: GRAND SLAM OFFERS

### Offer Defined
- **Name:** Offer Defined
- **What it is for:** Defines the offer as the start of getting customers and making money.
- **Parts or steps:**
  1. The offer begins the process of getting customers and making money.
  2. It is the first thing any new customer interacts with in your business.
  3. Core line: "Make people an offer so good they feel stupid saying no."
- **Rules and numbers:** None beyond the definition.
- **Fits piece type:** Any; foundational framing (judgment).
- **Copywriter uses it by:** Framing any offer piece around the "feel stupid saying no" standard (judgment).
- **Editor checks:** Does the piece present an actual offer, not just a service description? (judgment)
- **Source:** $100M Offers, "Offer Defined"

### Grand Slam Offer
- **Name:** Grand Slam Offer
- **What it is for:** An offer presented to the market that cannot be compared to any other product or service, so the cash constraint on growth is removed.
- **Parts or steps:** (the five components named in the definition)
  1. An attractive promotion
  2. An unmatchable value proposition
  3. A premium price
  4. An unbeatable guarantee
  5. A money model (payment terms) that lets you get paid to acquire customers
- **Rules and numbers:** It lets you sell in a "category of one" or "sell in a vacuum": the buyer's decision is between your product and nothing, so price is judged against nothing else. Result named by the author: more customers, at higher ticket prices, for less money. Because price cannot be compared, it re-calibrates the prospect's value-meter. (pp. 26-27)
- **Fits piece type:** Offer; long-form sales page (judgment).
- **Copywriter uses it by:** Checking that a draft offer carries all five components and stands alone without a competitor comparison (judgment).
- **Editor checks:** Are all five components present or deliberately addressed? Does the piece avoid inviting price comparison?
- **Source:** $100M Offers, Section I, p. 26; Section II "Value-Driven vs. Price-Driven Purchases," pp. 26-27

### Grand Slam Offer, example structure
- **Name:** Grand Slam Offer example
- **What it is for:** Shows the five components in practice (one-line description of the example).
- **Parts or steps:** Example: the sample stacks: pay one time (no recurring fee or retainer) and cover only ad spend; the provider generates and works the leads; payment only if people show up; guarantee of 20 people in the first month or the next month is free; plus the full industry playbook (daily sales coaching, tested scripts, tested price points and offers, sales recordings) free for becoming a client.
- **Rules and numbers:** Shows a guarantee in "if not X in Y, then Z" form and payment tied to results.
- **Fits piece type:** Offer pitch (judgment).
- **Copywriter uses it by:** Using it as a shape: payment tied to outcome, guarantee, free playbook (judgment).
- **Editor checks:** Does the draft state what the buyer pays, what they get, and what happens if it fails?
- **Source:** $100M Offers, Section I, pp. 29-30

## SECTION II: PRICING

### Only Two Ways to Grow
- **Name:** Only Two Ways to Grow
- **What it is for:** States the only growth levers in a business.
- **Parts or steps:**
  1. Get more customers
  2. Increase each customer's value
     - a. Increasing profit per purchase
     - b. Increasing the number of times they buy
- **Rules and numbers:** The author says a Grand Slam Offer covers all three growth requirements: more customers, higher payment, and more repeat purchases (stated in Section II "Value-Driven vs. Price-Driven Purchases").
- **Fits piece type:** Short-form video, LinkedIn post (judgment).
- **Copywriter uses it by:** Framing a growth point as one of the two levers and naming which sub-lever (judgment).
- **Editor checks:** Is the claim tied to a specific lever?
- **Source:** $100M Offers, Section II "Pricing: The Commodity Problem"

### Business Terms: Gross Profit and Lifetime Value
- **Name:** Gross Profit; Lifetime Value (LTGP, Lifetime Gross Profit)
- **What it is for:** Definitions and formulas the author uses for pricing.
- **Parts or steps:**
  1. Gross Profit = revenue minus the direct cost of servicing an ADDITIONAL customer.
  2. Lifetime Value = gross profit accrued over the entire life of a customer = gross profit multiplied by the number of purchases an average customer makes in their lifetime.
- **Rules and numbers:** Gross profit is not net profit; net profit is what is left after all expenses, not just direct fulfillment costs. The author counts gross profit, not total revenue, in lifetime value, and also calls it LTGP. Example: $1,000/mo service, $100/mo to fulfill, so 90% gross profit; a five-month customer gives $4,500 lifetime value.
- **Fits piece type:** LinkedIn post, long-form explainer (judgment).
- **Copywriter uses it by:** Defining the term in the author's sense before using any lifetime value number (judgment).
- **Editor checks:** Is "lifetime value" stated as gross profit, not revenue? Is gross profit not confused with net profit?
- **Source:** $100M Offers, Section II "Business Terms"

### Value-Driven vs. Price-Driven Purchases
- **Name:** Commoditized vs. Differentiated (Price-Driven vs. Value-Driven)
- **What it is for:** Explains why a Grand Slam Offer avoids price competition.
- **Parts or steps:**
  1. Commoditized = Price Driven Purchases ("race to the bottom").
  2. Differentiated = Value Driven Purchases (sell in a category of one with no comparison).
- **Rules and numbers:** A commodity offer competes on price. A Grand Slam Offer makes the business "appear as if it has a totally different product" and forces the prospect to assess value differently. (pp. 26-27)
- **Fits piece type:** LinkedIn post, long-form (judgment).
- **Copywriter uses it by:** Contrasting a price-comparison framing with a value framing (judgment).
- **Editor checks:** Does the draft avoid comparing price against competitors?
- **Source:** $100M Offers, Section II "Value-Driven vs. Price-Driven Purchases," pp. 26-27

### Finding the Right Market (A Starving Crowd): Four Indicators
- **Name:** Finding the Right Market (A Starving Crowd); the four indicators
- **What it is for:** Choosing a market before building an offer.
- **Parts or steps:**
  1. Massive Pain: they must not want, but desperately need, what you offer.
  2. Purchasing Power: the audience can afford what you charge.
  3. Easy to Target: you can target the ideal audience easily.
  4. Growing: growing markets are a tailwind, declining markets a headwind.
- **Rules and numbers:** "We are not trying to create demand. We are trying to channel it." Without a market for the offer, nothing that follows will work. A "normal" market grows at the same rate as the marketplace and has common unmet needs in one of three categories: improved health, increased wealth, or improved relationships. Degree of pain is proportional to the price you can charge. Key line: "The pain is the pitch." If you accurately articulate the prospect's pain, they will almost always buy. A prospect must have a painful problem to solve and charge for. When they hear the solution to the pain, and what life looks like without it, they should be drawn to it. (pp. 33-36; Section I "Massive Pain," p. 35)
- **Fits piece type:** Short-form video, LinkedIn post, niche research (judgment).
- **Copywriter uses it by:** Opening with the audience's pain stated accurately before any solution (judgment).
- **Editor checks:** Is the pain named specifically? Is the audience one that can pay?
- **Source:** $100M Offers, Section II "Finding the Right Market," pp. 33-36; Section I "Massive Pain," p. 35

### Starving Crowd (market) > Offer Strength > Persuasion Skills
- **Name:** Starving Crowd (market) > Offer Strength > Persuasion Skills
- **What it is for:** Ranks what matters most to results.
- **Parts or steps:**
  1. Starving Crowd (market)
  2. Offer Strength
  3. Persuasion Skills
- **Rules and numbers:** Rate each as great, normal, or bad and move left to right. A "great" on a higher item overpowers lower items. A "normal" passes the decision to the next item. A "bad" stops the equation unless a higher "great" nullifies it. Three cases: (1) bad offer and bad persuasion still make money in a great market; (2) normal market plus a Grand Slam Offer (great) makes money even with bad persuasion, "most of us"; (3) normal market plus normal offer requires exceptional persuasion, the hardest path. (pp. 37-38)
- **Fits piece type:** LinkedIn post, long-form (judgment).
- **Copywriter uses it by:** Using the order to justify fixing market and offer before copy (judgment).
- **Editor checks:** Does the draft keep the order market, offer, persuasion?
- **Source:** $100M Offers, Section II, pp. 37-38

## SECTION III: VALUE, CREATE YOUR OFFER

### The Value Equation
- **Name:** The Value Equation
- **What it is for:** The four drivers of value in an offer.
- **Parts or steps:**
  1. (Yay) The Dream Outcome (Goal: Increase)
  2. (Yay) Perceived Likelihood of Achievement (Goal: Increase)
  3. (Boo) Perceived Time Delay Between Start and Achievement (Goal: Decrease)
  4. (Boo) Perceived Effort & Sacrifice (Goal: Decrease)
- **Rules and numbers:** Two drivers (top) are increased; two (bottom) are decreased. "Get The Bottom To Zero": anyone can make a promise, so big claims on the top side are easy and less unique (the author calls beginner big-claim marketing easy and lazy); the harder, more competitive work is Time Delay and Effort & Sacrifice. The best companies focus on the bottom side: immediate, seamless, effortless. If time delay and effort and sacrifice are both zero, the product is "infinitely valuable," and "you win the game." (p. 57)
- **Fits piece type:** Offer, long-form, LinkedIn post, short-form video (judgment).
- **Copywriter uses it by:** Scoring a draft against all four drivers and strengthening the bottom two (judgment).
- **Editor checks:** Does the draft address time delay and effort, not just the dream outcome? Does it avoid empty big claims?
- **Source:** $100M Offers, Section III "The Value Equation," p. 57

### Value Equation driver 1: Dream Outcome
- **Name:** Dream Outcome
- **What it is for:** The expression of the feelings and experiences the prospect has envisioned; the gap between current reality and dreams.
- **Parts or steps:** People generally want:
  1. To be perceived as beautiful
  2. To be respected
  3. To be perceived as powerful
  4. To be loved
  5. To increase their status
- **Rules and numbers:** The goal is not to create desire but to channel it through the offer. The driver matters most when comparing two different desires: the one that more directly raises status is valued more. Talk in terms of things the prospect believes raise status. Depict the dream back accurately so the prospect feels understood, then explain how your vehicle gets them there. The dream outcome that most directly increases status is the one valued most. "It's not about the money, it's about the status" (relative standing socially or professionally). Pro Tip: frame benefits as status gained from the viewpoint of others. (p. 61)
- **Fits piece type:** Ad, short-form video, LinkedIn post (judgment).
- **Copywriter uses it by:** Writing the benefit as how other people will perceive the prospect's result (judgment).
- **Editor checks:** Is the benefit framed in status, as seen by others? Is the outcome the destination, not the process?
- **Source:** $100M Offers, Section III, p. 61

### Value Equation driver 2: Perceived Likelihood of Achievement
- **Name:** Perceived Likelihood of Achievement
- **What it is for:** Raises the prospect's conviction the offer will work for them.
- **Parts or steps:** Communicate it through:
  1. Messaging
  2. Proof
  3. What you choose to include or exclude in the offer
  4. Guarantees
- **Rules and numbers:** More conviction makes the offer more valuable even though the work on your end is unchanged. (p. 62)
- **Fits piece type:** Any offer piece; proof-led posts (judgment).
- **Copywriter uses it by:** Placing proof and a guarantee next to each outcome claim (judgment).
- **Editor checks:** Does each outcome claim have proof or a guarantee beside it?
- **Source:** $100M Offers, Section III, p. 62

### Value Equation driver 3: Time Delay
- **Name:** Time Delay (long-term outcome and short-term experience)
- **What it is for:** Shortens the time between buying and receiving the promised benefit.
- **Parts or steps:**
  1. Long-term outcome: the thing people buy (the dream outcome).
  2. Short-term experience: milestones along the way; the thing that makes them stay long enough to get the outcome.
- **Rules and numbers:** The shorter the distance between purchase and value, the more valuable the offer. Aim for a big emotional win early, as close to purchase as possible. Pro Tip: "Fast Wins." Always build in short-term, immediate wins so clients know they are on the right path. (p. 63)
- **Fits piece type:** Offer, onboarding email, lead magnet (judgment).
- **Copywriter uses it by:** Naming the first win and when it lands (judgment).
- **Editor checks:** Does the draft state an early win, not only the end result?
- **Source:** $100M Offers, Section III, p. 63

### Value Equation driver 4: Effort & Sacrifice
- **Name:** Effort & Sacrifice
- **What it is for:** What it "costs" people in ancillary costs, tangible and intangible, along the way.
- **Parts or steps:** None listed beyond tangible and intangible costs.
- **Rules and numbers:** Reducing real or perceived effort and sacrifice can massively boost the appeal of an offer. The same outcome sold by different vehicles carries very different value. Goal statement: increase the dream outcome and its perceived likelihood, decrease time delay and effort and sacrifice. (pp. 65-66)
- **Fits piece type:** Ad, short-form video (judgment).
- **Copywriter uses it by:** Naming the effort or pain the buyer avoids ("tired of...") (judgment).
- **Editor checks:** Does the piece say what the buyer no longer has to do?
- **Source:** $100M Offers, Section III, pp. 65-66

### Creating Your Grand Slam Offer, Part I: Problems & Solutions (Steps 1 to 3)
- **Name:** Creating Your Grand Slam Offer Part I: Problems & Solutions
- **What it is for:** Building the list of problems and solutions the offer must cover.
- **Parts or steps:**
  1. Step #1: Identify Dream Outcome
  2. Step #2: List Problems
  3. Step #3: Solutions List (two steps: transform problems into solutions, then name the solutions)
- **Rules and numbers:**
  - Step 1: The dream outcome is the buyer arriving at the destination and what they want to experience. Example: "Lose 20lbs in 6 weeks" (big outcome plus decreased time delay).
  - Step 2: Think about what happens immediately before and immediately after someone uses your product; what is the "next" thing they need help with? List problems in the sequence the customer meets them, in "insane detail." The more problems the better.
  - Each problem has four negative elements, matched to the four value drivers: (1) Dream Outcome: "This will not be financially worth it"; (2) Likelihood of Achievement: it will not work for me specifically, I will not stick with it, external factors will interfere (the most unique and service-specific bucket); (3) Effort & Sacrifice: it will be too hard or confusing, I will not like it, I will be bad at it; (4) Time: it will take too long, I am too busy, it is not convenient. (p. 78)
  - Count: 16 core problems with two to four sub-problems each, so 32 to 64 problems. The buckets only get the brain going; do not let them constrain the list. For each core thing a person must do, list all the reasons they could not do it or keep doing it.
  - Step 3: Ask "What would I need to show someone to solve this problem?" and reverse each obstacle into solution language. Adding "how to" and reversing the problem is a start. This becomes the checklist of what you must solve. (p. 78)
  - Example: "...is expensive" becomes "How to buy healthy food for less than your current grocery bill."
- **Fits piece type:** Offer building; lead magnet and content topic generation (judgment).
- **Copywriter uses it by:** Listing the buyer's sequential problems and flipping each into a "How to" headline or hook (judgment).
- **Editor checks:** Is each hook a specific problem flipped into a "how to"? Does the draft cover more than one of the four problem types?
- **Source:** $100M Offers, Section III "Creating Your Grand Slam Offer Part I," pp. 76-80

### Creating Your Grand Slam Offer, Part II: Trim & Stack (Steps 4 and 5)
- **Name:** Creating Your Grand Slam Offer Part II: Trim & Stack
- **What it is for:** Turning the solutions list into delivery vehicles, trimming, and bundling.
- **Parts or steps:**
  1. Step #4: Create Your Solutions Delivery Vehicles ("The How")
  2. Step #5: Trim & Stack
  3. Step #5b: Put all the bundles together into the ultimate high value deliverable
- **Rules and numbers:**
  - Sales to Fulfillment Continuum: find the sweet spot where you sell something very well that is also easy to fulfill. "Create flow. Monetize flow. Then add friction." Generate demand first, get yes with the offer, and only then add friction in marketing or offer less for the same price. Create cash flow by over-delivering at first, then use that cash to fix operations. (p. 85)
  - Step 4: the most important step. Think of anything you could do for each problem (divergent thinking), so good the buyer says "All that? Seriously? Yes, I'm in." You do this once for a product that may last years: high-value, high-leverage work, "you ultimately get paid for thinking." (p. 85)
  - Step 4 rule: solve every problem. "Don't get romantic about how you want to solve the problem." You must resolve every obstacle the buyer believes they will face to convert the most people; the goal is to sell the most people at the highest price with the highest margin. (pp. 88-89)
  - Step 5: look at the cost to you. Remove high cost, low value first. Then remove low cost, low value. If unsure what is high value, ask which items the person will: (1) financially value; (2) cause them to believe they will likely succeed; (3) make them feel they can do it with much less effort and sacrifice; (4) help them see the result with far less time investment. What should remain: (1) low cost, high value and (2) high cost, high value. (p. 90)
  - Focus on high-value "one to many" solutions. They take upfront work but become assets that create value in perpetuity at high margin. (p. 90)
  - Step 5b format: show each as Problem, then Solution Wording, then Sexier Name for Bundle, with the delivery vehicles listed beneath and a dollar value stated. Example: "Foolproof Bargain Grocery System" ($1,000 value). (pp. 91-94)
  - The bundle does three things: (1) solves all the perceived problems, not just some; (2) gives conviction that what you sell is one of a kind; (3) makes it impossible to compare or confuse your offer with the one down the street. (pp. 91-94)
  - Summary point: the process yields an offer that is differentiated and cannot be compared, so the prospect decides on value, not price. (p. 95)
- **Fits piece type:** Offer; long-form sales page; lead magnet (judgment).
- **Copywriter uses it by:** Giving each bundled solution a benefit-named title with a stated value (judgment).
- **Editor checks:** Does every named problem have a solution in the stack? Is a dollar value attached to each bundle?
- **Source:** $100M Offers, Section III "Creating Your Grand Slam Offer Part II," pp. 85-95

### Product Delivery Cheat Codes
- **Name:** Product Delivery Cheat Codes
- **What it is for:** Prompts for generating different ways to deliver each solution.
- **Parts or steps:**
  1. Personal attention level: one-on-one, small group, one to many.
  2. Effort expected from the buyer: do it themselves (DIY), do it with them (DWY: you teach them), done for them (DFY).
  3. Live delivery: which environment or medium (in-person, phone, email, text, Zoom, chat support).
  4. Recorded delivery: how they consume it (audio, video, or written).
  5. Reply speed: how quickly, on which days, during which hours (24/7, 9-5, within 5 minutes, within an hour, within 24 hrs).
  6. 10x to 1/10th test: what would I provide at 10x the price (or $100,000)? At 1/10th the price, how would I still make the product more valuable and the customer successful? Stretching both ways yields widely different solutions.
- **Rules and numbers:** Solve every problem; one item can be the reason someone does not buy. (pp. 88-89)
- **Fits piece type:** Offer building (judgment).
- **Copywriter uses it by:** Using the DIY/DWY/DFY lens to describe what the buyer does versus what Brett does (judgment).
- **Editor checks:** Is it clear whether the buyer does it, learns it, or has it done for them?
- **Source:** $100M Offers, Section III, pp. 88-89

## SECTION IV: ENHANCING YOUR OFFER

### Scarcity (Quantity): Three Types of Scarcity
- **Name:** Scarcity (Quantity); Three Types of Scarcity
- **What it is for:** Fixed supply creates fear of missing out and drives action and price.
- **Parts or steps:**
  1. Limited Supply of Seats/Slots (in general or over X period of time)
  2. Limited Supply of Bonuses
  3. Never available again
- **Rules and numbers:** "Fear of loss is stronger than desire for gain." Always sell out; it is better to sell out consistently than oversell and fail to create scarcity. Tell everyone you sold out: it gives social proof, and people who were on the fence want it more next time. (pp. 106-107)
- **Fits piece type:** Ad, email, offer, short-form video (judgment).
- **Copywriter uses it by:** Stating the real cap and what has been taken (judgment).
- **Editor checks:** Is the limit real and stated?
- **Source:** $100M Offers, Section IV "Scarcity (Quantity)," pp. 106-107

### Scarcity when Offering Services
- **Name:** Scarcity when Offering Services
- **What it is for:** Applying quantity limits to a service business.
- **Parts or steps:**
  1. Total Business Cap: only accepting X clients at this level of service (on-going); keep a waiting list so that when a spot opens, prospects jump in and price resistance disappears; periodically raise capacity by 10-20% then cap again; works for the highest tiers.
  2. Growth Rate Cap: only accepting X clients per week (on-going); tell prospects how many openings remain.
  3. Cohort Cap: only accepting X clients per class or cohort, on any cadence (monthly or quarterly starts).
- **Rules and numbers:** Always leave some demand unmet. Raise prices over time and squeeze out lower-performing accounts, or periodically open slots. (pp. 107-108)
- **Fits piece type:** Offer, email, LinkedIn post (judgment).
- **Copywriter uses it by:** Choosing one of the three caps and stating it in the call to action (judgment).
- **Editor checks:** Which of the three caps is used? Is the number stated?
- **Source:** $100M Offers, Section IV, pp. 107-108

### Limited Access for Higher Ticket Services; Honest Scarcity; Extreme Scarcity
- **Name:** Provide Limited Access For Higher Ticket Services; Honest Scarcity; Extreme Scarcity
- **What it is for:** Scarcity rules for upsells, capacity messaging, and a premium tier.
- **Parts or steps:** None numbered.
- **Rules and numbers:**
  - Pair workshops, trainings, events, seminars, consulting with fixed seats. Always have fewer spots than you think you can sell so people remember you sold out fast; it compounds. (p. 109)
  - Honest Scarcity: define the number of clients you will take in a period and advertise it. Saying you are three-fourths of the way to capacity, or 81% to capacity, moves people. Scarcity implies social proof, and only you decide where "full" is. (p. 109)
  - Extreme Scarcity: sell a very limited supply of 1-on-1 access (direct message, email, phone, voice memo, Zoom), cap at a tiny number, price very high, tell people. Deliver through a medium you do not hate. (p. 110)
  - Summary: use one or multiple scarcity methods; tell prospects your limits. (p. 110)
- **Fits piece type:** Offer, email, ad (judgment).
- **Copywriter uses it by:** Quoting capacity as a real fraction filled (judgment).
- **Editor checks:** Is the capacity figure truthful and specific?
- **Source:** $100M Offers, Section IV, pp. 109-110

### Urgency (Time): 4 Types of Urgency
- **Name:** Urgency (Time); 4 Types of Urgency
- **What it is for:** A defined deadline or cutoff creates urgency. Scarcity is a function of quantity; urgency is a function of time (limits when people can sign up, not how many).
- **Parts or steps:**
  1. Rolling Cohorts (Cohort-Based Rolling Urgency): start clients on a schedule (even weekly, in unlimited numbers) and say "sign up today to start Monday, otherwise wait for the next kickoff."
  2. Rolling Seasonal Urgency: show promotion dates and countdowns on landing pages and copy; rename the promotion by season so it has a start and finish. (p. 113)
  3. Promotional or Pricing Urgency (Pricing or Bonus-Based): the promotion, discount, or bonuses are what they could miss, not the service; swap pricing promotion, discount, or added bonuses (free install, free onboarding, extra workshop). (p. 114)
  4. Exploding Opportunity: the opportunity itself decays over time; every second of delay costs disproportionate gains; stops prospects waiting for a better offer. (p. 115)
- **Rules and numbers:** The less frequently you start clients, the stronger cohort urgency is. "Deadlines drive decisions." Pro Tip: "Clean Your Pipeline With Every Price Change." Tell people the price is going up. Never raise prices without telling people; it shows strength and brings cash from people on the fence. Pricing or bonus urgency lets year-round sellers keep urgency while keeping their integrity. (p. 114)
- **Fits piece type:** Email, ad, landing page (judgment).
- **Copywriter uses it by:** Selecting one of the four types and putting an actual date in the copy (judgment).
- **Editor checks:** Is there a real, dated deadline? Is the urgency one of the four types?
- **Source:** $100M Offers, Section IV "Urgency (Time)," pp. 113-115

### Bonuses
- **Name:** Bonuses (stack, order, and presentation)
- **What it is for:** Breaking one offer into component parts stacked as bonuses to widen the price-to-value gap.
- **Parts or steps:**
  1. A single offer is less valuable than the same offer broken into components and stacked as bonuses.
  2. Establish the price, anchored to the core offer.
  3. Expand with increasingly valuable bonuses so the price-to-value discrepancy widens ("but wait...there's more!").
- **Rules and numbers:** Increase value delivered rather than cutting price. Things you do but have not enumerated stay "unknown." (p. 117)
- **Fits piece type:** Offer, sales page, email (judgment).
- **Copywriter uses it by:** Listing each component as a named item with a value (judgment).
- **Editor checks:** Is each component listed and valued separately?
- **Source:** $100M Offers, Section IV "Bonuses," p. 117

### Add Bonuses Instead of Discounting; Presenting Bonuses 1-on-1
- **Name:** Add Bonuses Instead of Discounting Whenever Possible on Core Offers; Presenting Bonuses 1-on-1 Selling
- **What it is for:** Closing without lowering price.
- **Parts or steps:**
  1. Ask for the sale first, before offering bonuses.
  2. If they say yes, tell them the additional bonuses after they sign up (a wow experience that reinforces the decision).
  3. If they do not buy, present a bonus that matches their perceived obstacle, then ask again.
  4. Agree with the prospect, add the bonus, ask if this was "Fair enough"; repeat with another bonus.
- **Rules and numbers:** "Never discount the main offer." It teaches customers that prices are negotiable. Adding bonuses puts you in a position of strength; people find reciprocity hard to reject. (p. 118)
- **Fits piece type:** Sales conversation script, email (judgment).
- **Copywriter uses it by:** Scripting the bonus response to each named objection (judgment).
- **Editor checks:** Does the draft avoid discounting the core offer?
- **Source:** $100M Offers, Section IV "Bonuses," p. 118

### Bonus Bullets
- **Name:** Bonus Bullets
- **What it is for:** A checklist for writing each bonus.
- **Parts or steps:**
  1. Always offer them (use the bulleted bundle from the end of Section III).
  2. Give them a special name that has a benefit in the title.
  3. Tell them: a. how it relates to their issue; b. what it is; c. how you found it or what you did to create it; d. how it will specifically improve their lives or experience (faster, easier, or less effort/sacrifice per the value equation).
  4. Provide some proof (a stat, a past client, or personal experience).
  5. Paint a vivid mental image of their life after using it and experiencing the benefits.
  6. Always ascribe a price tag to them and justify it.
  7. Tools and checklists are better than additional trainings (lower effort and time, so higher value; the value equation still rules).
  8. Each should address a specific concern or obstacle about why the prospect can't or won't succeed (the bonus should prove the belief incorrect).
  9. It can also be what they will logically need next; solve the next problem before they meet it.
  10. Total bonus value should exceed the core offer's value; each added offer widens the price-to-value discrepancy.
  11. Enhance bonuses with scarcity and urgency: a. Bonuses With Scarcity (Version 1: only people who sign up for the program get Bonus 1, 2, 3, never sold elsewhere; Version 2: "I have 3 tickets left to my $5,000 virtual event," buy the program to get one); b. Bonuses With Urgency (Version 1: buy today and get a bonus that normally costs $1,000 free, "to reward action takers"); c. the difference: the scarcity examples are not time-constrained; the urgency one is about buying today or losing the bonuses.
- **Rules and numbers:** See each item. Numbering in the source came through garbled; the order above follows the file's sequence.
- **Fits piece type:** Offer stack, sales page, email (judgment).
- **Copywriter uses it by:** Running each bonus through the 11 items as a template (judgment).
- **Editor checks:** Does each bonus have a benefit-named title, a price tag, and a stated obstacle it removes?
- **Source:** $100M Offers, Section IV "Bonuses," Bonus Bullets (no page printed; falls between p. 118 and p. 123)

### Advanced Level Bonuses and Bonus Summary
- **Name:** Advanced Level Bonuses: Other People's Products and Services; Bonus Summary
- **What it is for:** Adding value at no cost to you.
- **Parts or steps:**
  1. Get other businesses to give their services or products as bonuses in exchange for free exposure to your clients.
  2. Create checklists, tools, swipe files, scripts, templates, and anything that takes lots of time to create alone but is easy to use once created.
  3. Record every workshop, webinar, event, and interview and reuse them as bonuses when needed to answer a perceived obstacle.
  4. Negotiate group discounts and a referral commission with adjacent businesses serving your customer's next need (you bargain with the buying power of all your customers at once).
- **Rules and numbers:** With enough partner relationships you can justify your entire price in savings and true-to-price bonuses. Information products have high perceived value, low cost, and zero operational effort beyond a login. Tickets to virtual events and a fixed-cost higher service level (VIP service for a month, which doubles as an upsell) also work. Anything invested in once that can be given away endlessly fits. Purpose: bonuses expand the price-to-value discrepancy and get people to buy who otherwise would not. (p. 123)
- **Fits piece type:** Offer, partner outreach (judgment).
- **Copywriter uses it by:** Listing reusable assets as bonuses (judgment).
- **Editor checks:** Are bonuses cheap to deliver and costly for the buyer to recreate?
- **Source:** $100M Offers, Section IV "Bonuses," p. 123

### Guarantees: four types
- **Name:** Guarantees (reverse risk); four types
- **What it is for:** Risk is the single greatest objection; reversing it makes any offer more attractive, and the author says to spend a disproportionate amount of time on how to reverse it.
- **Parts or steps:**
  1. Unconditional: the strongest; a trial where they pay first then see if they like it. Gets many more to buy, with some refunds.
  2. Conditional: includes terms and conditions; be very creative; aim for "better than money back," matching their investment with an equal or higher perceived commitment. Make key success actions part of the condition. Ideal: 100% qualify and achieve the result so none take it. Given the choice of refund or the promised outcome, most take the outcome.
  3. Anti-Guarantee: explicitly state "all sales are final"; give a creative "reason why" showing a massive exposure or vulnerability on your part; important for consumable items or items that lose value once given.
  4. Implied Guarantees: any performance-based offer (rev share, profit share, triggers, ratchets, monetary bonuses); if I don't perform, I don't get paid, and a great job pays well; works only with transparent measurement and trust or control of compensation.
- **Rules and numbers:** A guarantee gets power from a conditional statement of the form: no X result in Y time period, then we do Z. Without the "or what," it sounds weak. Example: weak "We will get you 20 clients guaranteed" versus stronger "20 clients in 30 days or your money back plus advertising dollars spent." Reversing risk is the number one way to increase conversion; experienced marketers spend as much time on guarantees as on deliverables. To craft one, identify the client's biggest fears, pain, and perceived obstacles (what do they not want to happen if they pay, what are they most afraid of) and reverse them. (pp. 125-126; p. 136)
- **Fits piece type:** Offer, ad, sales page (judgment).
- **Copywriter uses it by:** Writing the "if X in Y, we Z" statement with the specific "or what" (judgment).
- **Editor checks:** Does the guarantee name X, Y, and Z? Does it answer the buyer's biggest fear?
- **Source:** $100M Offers, Section IV "Guarantees," pp. 125-126, 136

### Stacking Guarantees; Name Your Guarantee Something Cool
- **Name:** Stacking Guarantees; Name Your Guarantee Something Cool
- **What it is for:** Combining and naming guarantees.
- **Parts or steps:**
  1. Stack an unconditional guarantee with a conditional one (example: 30-day no-questions-asked plus a conditional triple-money-back 90-day).
  2. Stack two conditional guarantees around different or sequential outcomes (example: $10,000 by 60 days, $30,000 by 90 days, if you do things 1, 2, and 3).
  3. Name the guarantee with strong imagery instead of a vanilla word like "satisfaction."
- **Rules and numbers:** Sequential conditional guarantees "future pace" the prospect and shift risk from them to you. Generic (bad): "30 Day Money Back Satisfaction Guarantee." Good: a named, imagery-based guarantee. (pp. 125-126)
- **Fits piece type:** Offer, ad (judgment).
- **Copywriter uses it by:** Giving the guarantee a vivid name and a timeline of outcomes (judgment).
- **Editor checks:** Is the guarantee named with imagery, not "satisfaction"?
- **Source:** $100M Offers, Section IV "Guarantees," pp. 125-126

### Four named guarantee formats
- **Name:** [Unconditional] Satisfaction-Based Refund Guarantee; [Conditional] Outsized Refund Guarantee; [Conditional] Service Guarantee; [Conditional] Modified Service Guarantee
- **What it is for:** Specific guarantee structures and what the client gets.
- **Parts or steps:**
  1. [Unconditional] Satisfaction-Based Refund Guarantee: if not satisfied with the service at any time, request a refund for the program. Works with a best-case/worst-case close. Highest form; you must be good at fulfilling; works better at lower ticket and is risky at higher ticket and higher cost of fulfillment.
  2. [Conditional] Outsized Refund Guarantee: double or triple their money back, or a no-strings payment far more than they paid. For high-margin products; add a consumption condition (they must do several things to qualify). Fits when the prospect has a lot to do and, if they do it, failure is unlikely.
  3. [Conditional] Service Guarantee: you keep working free of charge until X is achieved. Guarantees the goal but removes time. Can be conditional on key actions (such as setting up a page, attending calls, showing up, reporting data).
  4. [Conditional] Modified Service Guarantee: another Y-long period of service or access free; Y should give at least twice the duration. Ties a specific duration to the extended work.
- **Rules and numbers:** The author's advice: start selling service-based guarantees or setting up performance partnerships; this makes all sales final (no refund fear) and commits you to customers' results. (p. 136)
- **Fits piece type:** Offer (judgment).
- **Copywriter uses it by:** Choosing the guarantee type that fits ticket size and fulfillment cost (judgment).
- **Editor checks:** Does the guarantee match the ticket size and the provider's ability to deliver?
- **Source:** $100M Offers, Section IV "Guarantees," p. 136 (and preceding pages)

### Naming: the M-A-G-I-C formula
- **Name:** M-A-G-I-C (the magic headline formula) for naming an offer
- **What it is for:** Naming the offer so ideal prospects are interested enough to act. You change the name ("wrapping paper"), not the offer, to refresh leads.
- **Parts or steps:**
  1. M: Make a Magnetic "Reason Why" (why the offer is happening, or what is in it for me; the author says to think like a fraternity party planner; examples: Free, 88% off, Giveaway, Spring, Summer, Back To School, Grand Opening, New Management, New Building, Anniversary, Halloween, New Year).
  2. A: Announce Your Avatar (who you are and are not looking for; as specific as possible but no more; go hyper local, "Not Baltimore but Towson, MD"; examples: Bee Cave Dentists, Rolling Hills Moms, Salon Owners).
  3. G: Give Them A Goal (the dream outcome; specific and tangible; examples: Pain Free, Celebrity Smile, Double Your Profit, First Client, 7 Figure, 100k).
  4. I: Indicate a Time Interval (how long results take; examples: "4 Hour," "21 Day," "6 Week," "3 Month").
  5. C: Complete With A Container Word (denotes a bundle, a system that cannot be held up to a commoditized alternative; examples: Challenge, Blueprint, Bootcamp, Intensive, Incubator, Masterclass, Program, Detox, Experience, Summit, Accelerator, Fast Track, Shortcut, Sprint, Launch, System, Workshop, Reset, Solution, Hack, Cheatcode; the source lists more).
- **Rules and numbers:** Not all components are mandatory; use three to five. Shorter and punchier is better; balance brevity and specificity. Order is flexible: pick what sounds punchier. Write names out and test them: run two to three best names in the ad campaign, note the winner, use it as the control against new names. Pro Tip: Find Time To Rhyme (rhymes stick; do not force it; example "Six-Pack Fast Track"). Pro Tip: Alliteration (most words start with the same letter or sound; example "Make Money Masterclass"). Pro Tip: Name Sub Items & Bonuses with the same formula to enhance their value. Example: "5 Clients in 5 Days Blueprint." (pp. 138-143)
- **Fits piece type:** Offer name, ad headline, lead magnet title, short-form video hook (judgment).
- **Copywriter uses it by:** Generating several offer names and headlines from the five parts and testing two or three (judgment).
- **Editor checks:** Does the name use three to five of the components? Is it short and testable?
- **Source:** $100M Offers, Section IV "Naming," pp. 138-143

### What Happens When Offers Fatigue
- **Name:** What Happens When Offers Fatigue
- **What it is for:** The order in which to vary an offer to keep lead flow consistent.
- **Parts or steps:**
  1. Change the creative (images and pictures in your ads)
  2. Change the body copy in your ads
  3. Change the headline, the "wrapper" of your offer (Example: "Free 6 Week Lean Challenge" to "Free 6 Week Tone Challenge")
  4. Change the duration of your offer
  5. Change the enhancer of your offer (your free/discount component)
  6. Change the monetization structure, the series of offers you give prospects, and the price points
- **Rules and numbers:** Most of the time the first handful need changing, again and again, without touching the bottom of the list. When ads fatigue, run the same ad with a different video or image; then change words; only then change the wrapper. The lower on the list, the more operationally heavy; exhaust the lighter ways first. (pp. 144-145)
- **Fits piece type:** Ad refresh, content planning (judgment).
- **Copywriter uses it by:** Refreshing in the listed order: creative, body copy, headline (judgment).
- **Editor checks:** Does a refresh change the lightest element first?
- **Source:** $100M Offers, Section IV "Naming," pp. 144-145
