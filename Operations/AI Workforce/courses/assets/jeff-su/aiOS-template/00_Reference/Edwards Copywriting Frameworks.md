# Edwards Copywriting Frameworks

_Working sales-copy reference for your products, emails, sales pages, headlines, bullets, and conversion copy. Read when drafting or auditing copy that needs to connect features to buyer emotion, urgency, belief, and action. ## F.R.E.D. (Your Ideal Customer Avatar)

Before writing any copy, define the buyer using F.R.E.D.:

- **F: Fears:** What keeps them up at night? What are they afraid of happening (or not happening)?
- **R: Results:** What specific outcomes do they want? What does success look like to them?
- **E: Expectations:** What do they expect from a solution? What past experiences shape their expectations?
- **D: Desires:** What do they deeply want beyond the surface result? (Status, freedom, confidence, recognition)

Then define their **PQR2:**
- **P: Problems:** What specific problems do they face daily?
- **Q: Questions:** What questions do they ask when searching for solutions?
- **R: Roadblocks:** What's stopped them from solving this before?
- **R: Results:** What tangible results would make them feel the investment was worth it?

**Worked example:** A F.R.E.D. for a productivity course might be a busy professional who fears falling behind their peers, wants to save time and look competent, expects practical (not theoretical) instruction, and desires the confidence that they're doing things the right way.

## Feature → Benefit → Meaning (FBM)

The core framework for moving beyond features to emotional connection. Every piece of copy should chain through all three layers:

- **Feature:** What something **is** (the factual attribute)
- **Benefit:** What it **does for you** (the practical outcome)
- **Meaning:** What it **means to you** (the emotional payoff, often tied to freedom: freedom to do what you want, or freedom to avoid what you don't want)

**Connector phrases:** "so you can" / "so that" (feature → benefit), "which means" (benefit → meaning).

**Example:** "One-piece set **so you can** keep all your wrenches together in one place, **which means** you'll never be left high and dry with the wrong wrench."

**Key principle: emotion creates motion.** People buy on emotion and justify on logic. Features and benefits supply the logical justification; meaning supplies the emotion that actually moves someone to act. Most copy stops too early: it talks about features, sometimes gets to benefits, and rarely reaches meaning. Meaning is where all the emotion is located. If your draft doesn't have a clear meaning layer, you stopped one level short of what sells.

**The "amp it up" directive:** Finding the meaning isn't enough. Once you identify it, turn up the volume. Intensify the feeling to either push the reader away from pain or pull them toward desire. A meaning stated neutrally ("you'll feel more confident") is weaker than one with emotional weight ("you'll never sit in a meeting again wondering if everyone else knows something you don't"). Don't just name the emotion; make the reader feel it.

**Self-check for any draft:** (1) Did I actually get to meaning, or did I stop at benefit and call it done? (2) Is the meaning stated with enough intensity to create motion, or is it flat? If either answer is no, the copy isn't finished.

**Where it applies:** Use FBM to structure a core promise sentence (feature = what you're teaching, benefit = what it does for the reader, meaning = how it changes their experience). It also applies to sales bullets, email hooks, and any copy that needs to connect what something is to why it matters.

## The Ultimate Bullet Formula

Bullets are the workhorses of sales copy. They build curiosity and give reasons to buy. Structure:

**Formula:** [Specific hook] + "which means" + [benefit to them]

**Types of bullets:**
- **How-to bullets:** "How to [do specific thing] without [common pain]"
- **Secret bullets:** "The hidden [thing] that [surprising result]"
- **Mistake bullets:** "The #1 mistake [audience] makes with [topic] (and how to fix it in [timeframe])"
- **Result bullets:** "[Specific number/result] using [method/tool]"
- **Curiosity bullets:** Hint at the content without revealing it, and make them need to know.

**Key principle:** Every bullet should make the reader think "I need to know that." If it doesn't create that pull, cut it.

## 3 Sales Formulas That Never Fail

### Formula 1: Problem, Agitate, Solve (PAS)
1. **Problem:** State the problem clearly (use F.R.E.D.'s exact language)
2. **Agitate:** Make it worse. "Which means you'll never..." Pour on the pain.
3. **Solve:** Introduce your product as the solution

### Formula 2: Before, After, Bridge (BAB)
1. **Before:** Paint their current painful situation
2. **After:** Paint the dream outcome (vivid, specific)
3. **Bridge:** Your product is the bridge between the two

### Formula 3: If...Then
1. **If** you're [experiencing specific problem/situation]...
2. **Then** you need [product], because [reason + benefit]

**When to use which:** PAS for longer-form sales copy and email sequences. BAB for landing pages and social proof stories. If...Then for quick hooks, ads, and email subject lines.

## 13 Parts of a Sales Letter

Use these as building blocks, stacked top to bottom:

1. **Pre-head:** Call out the specific audience ("Attention: professionals who use AI daily")
2. **Headline:** The single most important piece. Must stop them and make them read the next line.
3. **Sub-headline:** Expands on the headline, adds specificity
4. **Opening:** Hook them emotionally. Often a story or a bold statement.
5. **Problem:** State the problem in their words (from F.R.E.D.)
6. **Agitate:** Make the problem feel urgent and painful
7. **Solution:** Introduce the product/offer as the answer
8. **Bullets:** List of specific benefits and curiosity hooks (use bullet formula above)
9. **Credibility:** Why should they trust you? (relevant experience, track record, real results)
10. **Proof:** Testimonials, case studies, screenshots, results
11. **Offer summary:** What they get, laid out clearly (the stack)
12. **Guarantee:** Remove the risk
13. **Call to action:** Tell them exactly what to do next. Be specific and direct.

**Key insight:** You don't write a sales letter. You assemble it from these 13 blocks. This makes the task manageable and ensures nothing is missed.

## Short Sales Letter (Page Structure)

The condensed variant for video sales pages and shorter-form offers. Same principles as the 13-part letter, compressed into a page layout:

1. **Headline:** Same rules as the long-form headline: stop them, make them read the next line.
2. **Video:** The video sales letter itself.
3. **Buy button:** Placed directly under the video. Don't make them scroll to act.
4. **4-6 curiosity-inducing bullets:** Below the buy button. Use the bullet formula (specific hook + "which means" + benefit). These catch anyone who skipped or skimmed the video.
5. **Guarantee:** Remove the risk.
6. **Summary of what they get:** The offer stack, laid out clearly.
7. **Second buy button:** Repeat the CTA after the stack and guarantee.
8. **Closing copy:** Final push. Address remaining objections or add urgency.
9. **P.S.:** Restate the main benefits from the video sales letter. Second-most-read element on the page.

## Headline Formulas

Headlines do one job: get the next line read. Formulas that work:

- "How to [get desired result] without [common pain/objection]"
- "The [number] [things] every [audience] needs to [desired result]"
- "[Specific result] in [specific timeframe] (even if [common objection])"
- "Warning: Don't [do common thing] until you [read/see/know this]"
- "What [authority/successful person] knows about [topic] that you don't"

---

## Email Teaser Formulas

For launch emails and sequences:

- **Subject line = headline.** Same rules apply. Must create curiosity or state a benefit.
- **Body = one idea, one action.** Don't cram multiple asks into one email.
- **P.S. lines are the second-most-read part** of any email. Use them for urgency, bonuses, or restating the CTA.
- **Short paragraphs.** One to three sentences max. White space is your friend.

## Copy Rules for a Sophisticated Audience

If your audience is sophisticated, they've seen every marketing trick. Adjust copy accordingly:

- **Substance over hype.** Never promise what you can't deliver. Specifics beat superlatives.
- **Show, don't tell.** Screenshots, demos, and real examples > adjectives.
- **Respect their intelligence.** No "WAIT! Before you go..." popups. No fake countdown timers. No manufactured urgency.
- **Your voice carries.** The copy should sound like you, not a marketer. Warm, direct, transparent. Apply your universal voice rules from `Voice Principles.md`; add external-message structure when the copy is correspondence.
