# Audience Persona

_Cross-platform audience reference for your core external-facing content audience across YouTube, newsletter, LinkedIn, Instagram, sales copy, and course material. Read before producing external-facing content or defining a more specific viewer/customer persona. This is a worked EXAMPLE you adapt to your own audience, not finished data. Replace the specifics below with what your analytics and surveys actually show. Does not contain video-specific viewer personas, product-specific ideal-customer profiles (ICPs), or voice/tone rules (see `Voice Principles.md`)._

_See also: `Voice Principles.md` (how you should sound) | `Hook Psychology Meta-Patterns.md` (attention mechanics) | `Edwards Copywriting Frameworks.md` (sales-copy persuasion mechanics)._

This file is the starting point for audience decisions, not the ceiling.

**Video-specific viewer personas and product-specific ICPs narrow and deepen this profile for their specific context.** When developing one, reference this file for baseline assumptions (demographics, AI usage level, career stage), then override any dimension where the specific topic or product data points to a more precise audience. If a product ICP diverges significantly from this persona, that's signal, not error. Flag the divergence and use it to inform both the product strategy and future updates to this file.

> **How to use this template:** The persona below is a filled-in example for a creator whose audience is corporate knowledge workers learning AI. Keep the two-layer structure (Segment Definition, then Person Profile) and the section headers; swap the content for your own audience. Replace every percentage and demographic figure with numbers from your own analytics and surveys, and delete the placeholder figures once you have real data.

## Layer 1: Segment Definition

Layer 1 answers "who is my core audience?" with observable, filterable traits. Use this layer to decide whether a piece of content is aimed at the right person, and to catch audience drift before it happens.

### Persona Name

**Professionals who use AI daily but don't trust their own approach, and want a proven system to follow instead of figuring it out themselves.**

### Design Constraint

This person already uses AI (they're past the beginner stage), they don't want to architect their own system (they want to be told what to do), and they will act on clear instructions (they're not passive). The moment you present three options and say "pick what works for you," you've lost them. They came to you specifically so they wouldn't have to pick.

### Role Filter

The primary audience works in one of these functions:

- Operations / Project Management
- Marketing / Communications / Content Strategy
- Consulting / Freelance / Agency (corporate-adjacent, not solopreneurs)
- Finance / Analytics / Research
- Product Management

Secondary roles that show up consistently but in smaller numbers: education, L&D, sales/business development, healthcare administration.

### Career Stage

3-8 years in their current function. They have enough domain expertise that AI should enhance what they already know, and they're still hands-on-keyboard: producing deliverables, sitting in meetings they didn't schedule, reporting to a manager. They don't set AI strategy for their organization, but they're expected to have an opinion on it.

Typical titles: Marketing Manager, Operations Lead, Business Analyst, Project Coordinator, Senior Consultant, Product Manager.

### AI Usage Level

Specific behavioral markers:

- Uses AI 3-5 times per week minimum, often daily
- Has a paid subscription to at least one AI tool
- Primary use cases: research and analysis, writing and editing, ideation and brainstorming
- Starts every AI session from scratch. Re-explains who they are, what they need, what tone to use
- Has never built a reusable system: no custom instructions, no persistent workflows, no configured assistants
- Gets usable output only after several attempts per task; rarely succeeds on the first prompt
- Knows AI can do more than what they're getting from it, but can't diagnose what they're doing wrong

They are past "Should I use AI?" and stuck at "How do I make this actually work for me?"

### Org Context

Works at a company with 50+ employees. Uses corporate tools (Slack, Google Workspace or Microsoft 365, Asana or Monday or Jira). Has IT policies that may restrict which AI tools they can use. Their company has no formal AI training, or if it does, it was a one-time "intro to AI" session that didn't change anyone's behavior.

They are often the informal "AI person" on their team: the one colleagues come to when they want to know which tool to use or how to write a better prompt. This status is self-appointed and fragile. They maintain it by staying current, which is getting harder every month.

### Anti-Persona

Profiles to explicitly exclude. Each one represents a content drift risk. If your content starts serving any of these people, it's moving away from the core audience.

**Solopreneurs and creators building business systems.** They want AI to run their business operations (invoicing, client management, content pipelines). Their problems are real but their context is different: no team meetings, no corporate tool constraints, no manager to impress. Content that drifts toward "build your AI-powered business" loses the corporate knowledge worker.

**Developers and technical builders.** They evaluate AI from first principles, read documentation, and build automations with code. Content that includes API calls, config files, or terminal commands signals "this isn't for me" to the core audience.

**AI hobbyists and tool collectors.** They follow every launch, try every new tool, and watch AI content for entertainment. They're engaged viewers but poor conversion candidates. Content optimized for them trends toward "first look" reviews and feature showcases instead of practical workflows.

**Complete beginners who haven't used any AI tool.** They need "what is ChatGPT?" level orientation. Your content assumes they've already used AI and hit a wall. Beginners need a different on-ramp, and trying to serve them alongside the core audience forces basic explanations that bore the primary viewer.

## Layer 2: Person Profile

Layer 2 answers "what is this person's day-to-day life like?" in terms every content channel can use for tone, examples, and creative decisions. Use this layer when writing copy, picking scenarios, or calibrating how technical to go.

### Goals (Ranked by Intensity)

**1. Take on more ambitious work because AI handles the repetitive parts.**
They want to do work they currently can't attempt because the overhead is too high. Saving time matters, but it's a byproduct. The real goal is expanding what's possible.

_You'd hear them say:_ "I know I could take on that project if I wasn't spending half my day on formatting and first drafts."

**2. Build something they're proud of, not something they copied.**
There's a craft element. They want to have made something: a configured system, a workflow, a set of assets they can point to. This is about ownership, not just utility.

_You'd hear them say:_ "I want to actually understand how this works, not just copy someone else's prompts."

**3. Spend dramatically less time on repetitive work.**
Efficiency is real but it's a means to an end. It ranks below ambition and craft pride when measured as a desired outcome. The distinction matters for content: lead with what they'll be able to do, not how much time they'll save.

_You'd hear them say:_ "By the time I've done all that back-and-forth with the AI, I could have just done it myself."

**4. Feel confident and in control when using AI.**
They currently feel like they're guessing. Every session involves uncertainty about whether they're doing it "right." Confidence means knowing why their approach works, not only that it works.

_You'd hear them say:_ "I feel like everyone else has figured something out that I haven't."

**5. Be recognized as the person who "gets" AI on their team.**
Social authority is real but secondary. It's a consequence of the first four goals, not a standalone motivation. Content that leads with "become the AI leader" overweights this.

_You'd hear them say:_ "My colleague asked me what AI tool to use last week and I didn't have a good answer."

### Frustrations (Specific Scenarios)

These are moments, not categories. Use them to build relatable examples in content.

**Monday morning, fresh chat window.** They open an AI assistant to draft a client proposal. First 5 minutes: re-explaining their company, their role, the tone they want, the format they need. They've done this exact task before. But today they're starting from zero, and they can feel the time draining.

**The LinkedIn scroll.** They see a peer post about their "AI workflow" with a screenshot of a configured system. They don't know what half the terms mean. They feel intrigued and behind at the same time. They want to ask how to set it up but don't want to look uninformed.

**The new tool announcement.** A major AI vendor ships a big update. Their first reaction is dread, not excitement: "Do I have to relearn everything again?" They don't have a framework for evaluating whether this matters, so they either panic-watch a stack of videos or ignore it entirely. Both feel wrong.

**The tutorial dead end.** They find a video about AI productivity. It either shows a single impressive demo with no explanation of how to replicate it, or it's a developer-focused walkthrough with terminal commands and config files. There's nothing in between for them.

**The multi-prompt loop.** They paste a rough draft into AI and get back something generic. Re-prompt for tone. Re-prompt for format. Edit manually. Re-prompt again. After 20 minutes they have something usable, but they're not sure why it took that long or how to make it faster next time.

**The team meeting.** Someone asks "should we be using AI for this?" They give a vague answer because they don't have a framework for when AI helps vs. when it wastes time. They used to be the person everyone came to for tool recommendations.

### Information Diet

**Primary discovery channel:** YouTube. They search for specific tool names or problem-framed queries ("best AI tools for productivity"). They subscribe to a handful of AI-focused channels and watch irregularly, usually when a new tool or update drops.

**Secondary channels:** LinkedIn (peer validation, keeping up with what colleagues post about AI). Newsletter (read on mobile during commute). Instagram (occasional, not a primary learning channel for this audience).

**Channels they don't use:** Reddit, Hacker News, Discord, developer forums, X/Twitter AI discourse. These skew too technical or too noisy for them.

**Trusted voice profile:** They trust a practitioner who has done the work, not a reviewer who summarizes features or a theorist who explains concepts. Three credibility markers they look for: (1) the person uses the tool in real professional workflows, not demos, (2) the person is opinionated about what to use and what to skip, (3) the person has a track record of making complex tools accessible. They distrust content that leads with hype, showcases features without workflows, or hedges every recommendation with "it depends."

**Content consumption pattern:** They watch in focused bursts, usually when a specific need triggers a search. They don't browse AI content for fun. They want to watch one video, get what they need, and go apply it. They'll watch longer if the content is structured so they can see progress (capability-by-capability, feature-by-feature).

### The Quote

One sentence they'd say to a colleague at lunch:

> "I use AI every day and I still feel like I'm doing it wrong, but I don't have time to figure out the right way on my own."

### Team Leader Offshoot

Same person as the primary persona, with one additional characteristic: they want to bring AI competence to their team or organization. A meaningful share of this audience has a team dimension to their AI work (some lead a small team of 2-10, some a department or org of 10+).

**What makes them different from the primary persona:**

- They've seen the potential of AI for their team but can't get buy-in because they don't have a structured system to point to
- They're frustrated that colleagues either aren't using AI at all, or are producing obviously AI-generated work that makes the whole team look bad
- They want to be the person who brings AI competence to their organization, not someone who only uses it individually

**Content modifier:** When a piece of content naturally supports a "bring this to your team" angle (workflow tutorials, tool setup guides, system walkthroughs), include one concrete step for how they'd share or hand off the workflow. When the content doesn't support that angle (AI trends, tool comparisons), ignore this offshoot.

_You'd hear them say:_ "I'm on an island. My team either isn't using AI at all or giving me obviously AI-generated work."

## Usage Rules

### Who reads this file

Every channel that produces external-facing content: YouTube, newsletter, LinkedIn, Instagram, product/sales copy, external comms, partner content, speaking, workshops, and any visuals produced for external content.

### How to use this file

**Example selection.** When you need a relatable scenario, the Person Profile (Layer 2) specifies what tools they use, what their day looks like, and what frustrates them. Pull examples from their world, not yours.

**Tone calibration.** The Segment Definition (Layer 1) specifies their technical level (daily AI user, never built a reusable system) and their emotional state (overwhelmed, skeptical of hype, wants clear instructions). No jargon they'd need to Google. No "here are 5 options to choose from." No abstract frameworks without a concrete first step.

**Objection anticipation.** The Frustrations section tells you what they've already tried and why it didn't work. Content that falls into either the "too shallow" or "too technical" trap loses them. The anti-persona section tells you which audience drift patterns to watch for.

**Team leader modifier.** When content naturally supports a "bring this to your team" angle, reference the offshoot for framing. When it doesn't, ignore it.

### Relationship to viewer personas and product ICPs

This file is the outermost ring. Video-specific viewer personas and product ICPs are concentric circles within it.

**Viewer personas** narrow this persona for a specific video topic. This file answers "who is this person before they click on this video?" The viewer persona answers "what specific problem brought them to this video, and how does it transform them?" This file makes the viewer persona faster to develop (baseline demographics, career stage, and AI usage level are already established) but should never constrain its specificity. A video about meeting prep can narrow to "ops leads who run 6+ meetings a week" without conflicting with the master persona, because that person is a subset.

**Product ICPs** go deeper and may diverge. This file tells you who your audience is. The product ICP tells you which slice of that audience this product serves, what triggers them to buy, and what objections to handle. If survey data reveals that a product's actual buyers skew differently from the master persona (younger, more technical, more solopreneur), the product ICP reflects reality. This file stays unchanged unless the divergence reveals a permanent shift in your core audience.

### When to update this file

- When new analytics data shows a meaningful demographic shift
- When a product ICP reveals a consistent divergence that suggests the core audience is evolving
- When you make a strategic decision to shift the target audience
- During a periodic audit (check for staleness)
